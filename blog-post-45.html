PACKAGING DESIGN PRICING

How Much Does Packaging Design Cost in 2026?

November 26, 2026 • 13 min read • By Muhammad Atif Iqbal

Packaging is the only piece of design that must do three jobs at once, in a few seconds, while being picked up, turned over, and compared with its neighbours: it must protect the product inside it, it must explain what the product is and why it is worth buying, and it must be recognised again the next time the buyer sees it. A logo, a business card or a flyer can be judged on a screen. A pack cannot. It is judged in the hand, on a shelf, under shop lighting, beside packs that are trying to do exactly the same thing, by a buyer who has already decided, in broad terms, what kind of product they want, and who will choose between similar packs on the evidence in front of them. That combination is exactly why its price is so often misunderstood. From the outside, a pack can look like a label on a box: a name, a picture, a few details. In practice, a good pack is a piece of persuasion that has to work in three dimensions, in a fixed order of looking — front first, then the sides, then the back — with a structure that must fold, seal, open and survive handling, and with artwork that must be prepared so that the printer or manufacturer returns the pack the designer intended, with nothing cut off, folded over, or lost in a seam. This guide explains what packaging design cost actually covers in 2026, the general market ranges for design alone, how formats, sizes, variants, materials and finishes move the price, what a proper packaging design package includes, and how design, printing, materials, finishing and distribution interact, so that the total cost of a finished, delivered pack is clear before anyone is paid. One thing to be clear about up front: the ranges in this post are general market ranges, not quotes and not a promise of any price. Your exact price depends on your pack, your format and size, your materials and finishes, and your manufacturer or printer, and any designer worth hiring will quote your job in writing after reading your brief and seeing the dieline.

The reason the question matters is that packaging is, for many products, the last piece of marketing the buyer sees before deciding, and the first piece of the product they experience after buying. A website waits to be found, and a social media post waits to be shown, but a pack is placed in the buyer's hand at the moment they have already shown interest, and it is the thing they take home, open, use, and keep — or throw away. That role is what makes the design stage worth doing properly, even though the pack may be small. If the front is unclear, if the name cannot be read at shelf distance, if the weight, the flavour, the size or the use is hard to find, or if the pack looks crowded and cheap beside the packs it will sit next to, the business lives with that impression for the whole production run, because a printed pack cannot be edited after it leaves the manufacturer. That permanence, combined with the short time a pack gets to make its point on each face, is what makes the cheapest route risky: a pack assembled in a hurry from a generic template, with no check of the dieline, the folds or the back-panel wording, can look acceptable on a screen and disappointing in the hand, because a screen hides the very things a physical pack reveals — small text that cannot be read comfortably, a photograph that breaks up when printed large, a fold that lands across a headline, a seam that hides a warning, and a back panel that is so crowded the buyer cannot find the one detail they need. The useful question is therefore not "what is the cheapest pack?" but "what should I pay for a packaging design that will be noticed, understood in order, trusted, opened without frustration, and recognised again, and printed and finished well at the quantity I need?" The rest of this guide answers that question, starting with what packaging design actually is, and what it is not.

What Packaging Design Actually Is

Packaging design is the design of a three-dimensional container so that a buyer can understand it in order, trust it, and use it without difficulty. The designer decides the hierarchy: which piece of information is met first, which second, and which can wait until the buyer turns the pack over. In most cases the front comes first, because it is what earns the pick-up: the product name, the kind of product, the flavour, scent, size or variant, and the one promise, stated in a few words and one strong image that can be understood at a glance and at shelf distance. The sides come next, in the order the buyer meets them: the detail that supports the front — the ingredients, the contents, the use, or the story — each given the space it needs and no more. The back carries the information the buyer checks before buying and needs after buying: the full details, the instructions, the warnings, the barcode, and the contact or origin details, set large enough to read without hunting. A designer who understands hierarchy will resist the temptation to fill every face. Empty space on a pack is not wasted space. It is what lets the eye find the name, and it is what keeps the pack from looking like a website that has been wrapped around a box until it is unreadable. A pack that tries to say everything on every face will be read by nobody, because the buyer will conclude, in the first few seconds, that reading it would take too long, and will put it down and pick up the pack beside it.

What packaging design is not is a new brand identity, and it is not a label stuck on a box. A packaging design normally works with artwork and colours that already exist: the business name, its existing mark, its colours and its fonts, supplied by the client or taken from an earlier brand package. If the business does not yet have those things, the packaging job becomes larger, because the designer must first settle the colours, the fonts and the arrangement that the pack will then use — and that wider work is priced separately, as our companion post on how much a logo costs in 2026 explains for the identity side of the work. A pack is also not a flat design bent around a box. A flat layout that has not been built on the dieline will fail where the pack folds, where it seals, and where it is cut, because the flat artwork and the finished pack are different shapes, and the design must be planned for the finished shape from the start. That distinction matters when comparing quotes and when writing the brief. One quote may assume the client will supply the artwork, the wording, the barcode and the photographs, ready to lay out, and that the dieline is already agreed with the manufacturer, while another may include time to tidy the artwork, choose matching fonts, source or improve photographs, shape the wording so that it fits the faces, and agree the dieline with the manufacturer. Both can be fair quotes, but they are quotes for different jobs, and the cheaper one is not cheaper if the wording still needs to be written, the barcode still needs to be placed, or the dieline still needs to be agreed before the pack can be laid out. Ask, before you accept any quote, what the designer expects you to supply, and what the designer will prepare. The answer will tell you whether the price covers the pack artwork alone, or the pack artwork plus the preparation that makes the pack possible.

Label, Box, Pouch, Tube, Sleeve: Knowing Which Pack You Need

The word packaging covers several different kinds of pack, and the confusion costs money, because each one is a different job with a different price. A label is a printed face applied to a container — a bottle, a jar, a tin — that must say, on a curved or flat surface, what the product is, in the space the container allows, and must survive handling, moisture and the life of the product. A folding carton is a box, cut and folded from card, that must open, close, protect and stack, with a front, sides and back that work in order. A pouch or bag is a flexible pack, sealed at the edges, that must stand or hang, reseal where it should reseal, and show the product or the design clearly through the material chosen. A tube or a jar is a rigid container whose label or printed face must wrap correctly, with the seam and the opening planned from the start. A sleeve or wrap is a band around another pack or product, carrying the branding and the information for a pack whose own surface cannot carry them. The five are priced differently because they are designed differently. A label is one or two flat layouts, planned for a curve. A box is several faces planned on a dieline, with folds and glue areas. A pouch is a front and a back planned for a flexible material, with seals that must be kept clear. A tube or jar label must be planned for the wrap, so that the seam does not hide the name. A sleeve must be planned for the pack it wraps, so that it fits and does not slip. Before asking for a quote, decide which pack you need, by asking how the buyer will meet it, how it will be stored, and how it will be opened. If it will be picked up from a shelf and read at a glance, it is a front-led pack. If it will be stored in a cupboard and used daily, it is a pack that must be easy to open and close. If it will be posted, it is a pack that must survive the post. If it will be kept and refilled, it is a pack whose design must last. A designer who is told the wrong format will design a pack that looks striking and fails in use: a label that wraps badly, a box that will not close, or a pouch whose seal covers the name. The format should follow the product, the shelf and the use, not fashion, and the quote should follow the format.

Dielines, Structure and Materials: The Shape Before the Artwork

The most important document in a packaging job is not the artwork. It is the dieline: the flat template of the pack, showing the outline, the fold lines, the glue areas, the cut lines and the safe areas, drawn to the true size and shape of the finished pack. The designer lays the artwork out on the dieline, keeping text and important details clear of folds, cuts, seams and glue areas, and the manufacturer cuts, folds and assembles the pack from the same dieline. Where the printer or manufacturer supplies the dieline, the design work starts from a known shape, and the cost is the cost of the artwork on that shape. Where no dieline exists — for a new shape, a new size, a new material, or a pack made by a new supplier — the dieline must be agreed with the manufacturer before the artwork is finished, and that step takes time, may need a physical sample or prototype, and may add cost. A prototype, made from the dieline in the real material, is worth its cost for any pack that will be made in quantity, because it is the only way to check, before printing, that the pack folds as intended, that the front reads at shelf distance, that the back wording is not lost in a fold, and that the pack opens and closes as the buyer will expect. Structure and materials must be chosen together with the dieline. A card box, a corrugated shipper, a flexible pouch, a rigid tube and a glass jar each behave differently, each prints differently, and each suits a different product, a different shelf life, and a different way of opening. A material chosen for its look, without regard to how it prints, how it folds, or how it survives handling and storage, will produce a pack that looks good in a mockup and disappoints in the hand. Tell the designer and the manufacturer how the pack will be used, stored, posted and opened, before the structure and the material are chosen, because the use decides the structure, the structure decides the dieline, and the dieline decides part of the price. A pack that will be posted should be light, strong and flat where it can be. A pack that will sit on a shelf should stand, stack and be recognised at a distance. A pack that will be opened daily should open easily and close again. Each use points to a different structure, a different material, and a different price, and the choice should be made deliberately, once, before the artwork begins.

Copy, Photography and the Back Panel: The Work Before the Layout

A pack is only as good as the words and photographs it is given to lay out, and the work of preparing them is often the largest hidden part of a packaging job. A flyer needs a headline and a few details. A pack needs a front line, a product description, a back panel with the full details, instructions, warnings and origin information, a barcode placed where it can be scanned, and, for many products, regulated information that must appear in a stated form. Each of those must be written for the face the buyer will meet it on, in the order the buyer will meet it, not copied from a website in the order a website presents it. One quote may assume the client will supply the wording, the barcode, and the photographs, ready to lay out, while another may include time to write, shorten and shape the wording so that it fits the faces and reads in order. Both can be fair quotes, but they are quotes for different jobs, and the cheaper one is not cheaper if the wording still needs to be written, the barcode still needs to be supplied in the right form, or the regulated information still needs to be checked before the pack can be laid out. The same is true of photographs. A pack needs fewer photographs than a brochure, but each one must be sharp at the size it will be printed at, in the orientation the face needs, and honest about the product inside. A photograph that looks sharp on a phone screen can look soft or blocky when printed large on a pack if it was supplied too small, and a photograph supplied in the wrong orientation will either be cropped badly or leave the face with a gap it cannot fill. Where a product must show regulated information — ingredients, allergens, warnings, net contents, or other details that apply to its kind of product — the designer is not the person who decides what must appear. The client, or the client's adviser, must supply the exact wording and confirm that it is complete and correct, because a pack that omits required information, or states it wrongly, cannot be fixed by good design, and may have to be reprinted. Ask, before the work starts, who supplies the wording, who checks the regulated information, who supplies the barcode and in what form, and who supplies the photographs. The answers will tell you whether the price covers the pack artwork alone, or the pack artwork plus the preparation that makes the pack lawful and usable. The cheapest pack is rarely the one with the cheapest artwork. It is the one whose words, barcode and photographs were ready, in the right order, before the layout began, so the designer's time was spent designing rather than waiting, chasing, or rewriting.

Design Cost Versus Printing, Materials, Finishing and Distribution: Separate Bills

The most common confusion in packaging pricing is the belief that there is one price for a pack. In practice there are almost always several: the price of designing the pack, the price of the materials, the price of printing and making it, the price of finishing it, and the price of getting it to the buyer. The design fee is usually a one-off payment. It pays for the designer's time: understanding the brief, planning the hierarchy, arranging the content face by face on the dieline, choosing and setting the type, placing the artwork and the photographs, preparing the print-ready files, and making the agreed revision rounds. Once the design is approved and the files are delivered, the design fee is done, and a reprint of the same pack should not need a new design fee, unless the content changes. The materials and production fee is a per-run payment. It pays for the card, film, glass, metal or other material, the ink or print process, the cutting, folding, sealing or assembly, and the number of packs ordered, and it is paid again every time a new batch is made. Finishing is usually charged as part of production: foil, embossing, lamination, a special coating, a window, or a handle, each of which adds a step and a cost that grows with the quantity and the complexity of the pack. The distribution cost is the bill most often forgotten: the postage or freight for the finished packs, the cost of the outer cartons, the time spent packing and sending them, and the cost of storage before they are used. Some printers bundle a basic template layout with the print order, usually from a template library, and some designers include a first production run in their package price. Both arrangements are legitimate, but they must be read carefully. A bundled layout is not the same as a custom design, and a package that includes production is only good value if the material, finish, size and quantity it includes are the ones the business actually wants. Always ask, in plain words: does this price include the design, the materials, the printing and making, the finishing, the distribution, or all of them, and if it includes production, how many packs, on what material, with what finish and what size?

Separating the bills also makes the long-term cost clearer. A custom design that costs more up front can be the cheaper choice over several production runs, because the design is paid for once and the reprints cost only the materials, production and distribution. A cheap template layout that is tied to one printer, in one file format that cannot be taken elsewhere, can become expensive when the business changes printer, changes a detail, or wants the same pack in a different size, because the layout must then be remade. Distribution deserves the same care. A thousand packs that sit in storage because nobody was paid or given time to fill and send them are not cheap packs. They are wasted packs, and the money spent making them would have been better spent on a smaller run, properly used, and a second, updated run later. When you order the first run, ask how a reprint will work, what file will be used, and whether a small change — a new flavour, a new size, a new warning — will be charged as a small edit or as a new design. The answer is part of the true cost of the pack, even though it will not be paid until later. And when you compare quotes, compare design with design, materials with materials, production with production, finishing with finishing, and distribution with distribution. A quote that looks expensive because it includes a thousand finished packs is not comparable with a quote that prices the design alone. Put the parts side by side, in the same table, with the same quantity, the same material, the same finish and the same size, and the comparison will be honest. If a quote does not separate the parts, ask for the separation. A designer or manufacturer who will not separate them is asking you to buy without knowing what you are buying. Our companion post on how much brochure design costs in 2026 covers the printed document that often accompanies a product launch, and the two should be budgeted together where both are needed, so that the pack and the brochure match and the total is known before either is ordered.

How Quantity and Variants Change the Total

Quantity changes the total for packaging in a way that surprises many first-time buyers, because the relationship is not a straight line, and because variants make the curve steeper than for a single pack. The design fee does not change with quantity: designing a pack that will be made one hundred times costs the same as designing a pack that will be made five thousand times, because the artwork is the same. The production fee, however, falls sharply per pack as the quantity rises, because the fixed costs of setting up the job — preparing the files, setting the material, checking the first packs, setting the cutter and the folder — are spread across more packs. The first hundred packs therefore carry a high cost per pack, the next few hundred carry a much lower one, and by the time the run reaches a few thousand, the cost per pack is often a small fraction of what it was for the first hundred. Variants change that picture. A product in four flavours, three sizes, or two languages is not one pack made four times. It is four artworks, or four versions of one artwork, each of which must be laid out, checked and proofed, each of which needs its own back-panel wording, and each of which may need its own production set-up. A range that is planned as a system — the same front, the same hierarchy, the same back-panel arrangement, with only the flavour, colour or size changing — will cost less per variant than a range in which each variant is designed as if it were a new product, because the system, once settled, can be applied. A range that is not planned as a system will cost more, take longer, and look less consistent on the shelf, because each variant will have been designed separately, and the shelf will show it. The sensible quantity is therefore a judgement, not a calculation. A pack for a one-off launch, with a fixed date, should be made in the quantity that can be filled and sold before the date, with a small margin for the packs that will be damaged, and no more. A pack for an ongoing product, with no date on it, can justify a larger run, because the per-pack saving is real and the packs will be used before anything changes. A pack with details that may change — a price, a claim, a regulated wording — should be made in the quantity that can be used before the detail is likely to change, or the changing detail should be kept on a separate label or insert that can be replaced without remaking the whole pack. Get the production quote in writing, for the exact quantity, material, finish, size and number of variants, before approving the design. A design approved for a material, a finish or a quantity the budget cannot afford will have to be redesigned, and that redesign is the most avoidable cost in the whole packaging project.

General Market Ranges for Packaging Design

The ranges below are what you will typically see across freelance marketplaces, designer portfolios and studio pricing pages, for the design work on packaging. They are general market ranges, not quotes, and they do not include materials, printing, finishing, moulds, plates or distribution unless the text says so. The exact price for your pack depends on the format, the size range, the number of variants, whether the wording, barcode and photographs are supplied ready to use, whether a dieline already exists, how many design versions and revision rounds are included, and whether mockups or a prototype are also needed. Treat these as a map, not a price list, and always get a written quote for your exact pack.

Template or online print-service layout: roughly $0 to around $75, often bundled with printing. You choose a template, type your details into it, and the printer prints the result. The design work is selection and typing, not custom layout, and the result can be perfectly serviceable for a straightforward pack with a single variant and standard details: a simple label for a single product, or a plain box for a single item. The trade-offs are the ones that apply to any template: the layout is shared with other businesses, the fonts and spacing cannot be adjusted far, the photograph choices are limited, the size and shape are fixed by the template, and the file usually stays inside the printer's system, so a reprint elsewhere means starting again. This tier suits a pack that is needed urgently, a pack for a short-lived purpose, or a business that wants to test its wording before paying for a custom design. It does not suit a pack that must look distinct, use a new shape or material, carry several variants, or match an existing brand precisely.

Custom label design by a freelancer: roughly $75 to $300 for a single label. A designer lays out the label for this product alone, working from the supplied artwork, wording, barcode and photographs, with one or two design versions and one or two revision rounds, and delivers print-ready files built for the container's size and curve. This is the tier where most small-product labels land, and it is usually the best value for a single label: the layout is original, the front is set for the label's real size and reading situation, the back wording is arranged so it can be read, and the files are prepared for the printer and the material. The range is wide because a small flat label with supplied artwork and one revision round is a smaller job than a wraparound label with a curved surface, several languages, or a back panel with regulated information that must be set exactly. A label that needs the wording shortened and shaped, the photographs improved, or a mockup of the finished container will sit at the higher end of this tier, and that is fair, because each of those is real work. Check the portfolio for printed labels on real containers, not only flat mockups, and ask whether the price includes the print-ready files, the mockup, and the revision rounds you expect.

Custom box or pouch design by a freelancer: roughly $250 to $1,200 for a single format. A designer lays out the pack for this product alone, working on the final dieline, with one or two design versions and one or two revision rounds, and delivers print-ready artwork and a mockup of the finished pack. This is the tier where most small-business boxes and pouches land, and it is usually the best value for a standard pack: the layout is original, the faces work in order on the dieline, the files are prepared for the printer, the material and the finish, and the pack can be reprinted from those files. The range is wide because a single-size folding carton with supplied artwork and one revision round is a smaller job than a pouch with several sizes, several variants, a window, a resealable closure, or a finish that must be planned on the dieline. A pack that needs a new dieline agreed with the manufacturer, a prototype, or several variants built as a system will sit at the higher end of this tier, or above it, and that is fair, because each of those is real work. A range of several variants, each with its own back panel, will usually be quoted as a system price, with a first pack and a price per additional variant, rather than as several separate single-pack prices. Ask how variants are charged, and whether the price includes the dieline check, the mockup, and the revision rounds.

Packaging system within a brand or launch package: roughly $1,200 to $4,000, usually as part of wider work. A studio rarely prices a packaging system in isolation at this tier. The pack is designed as part of a launch — the brand identity, the social media images, the website, the brochure, the display — with a set process, more than one person checking the work, and a consistent look across every piece. The pack itself may be one item in a package that costs more, and judging it by the pack alone will make it look expensive. Judged as part of the launch, it is the cost of consistency: the same name, colours and images on the pack, the website and the launch materials, checked by a team. This tier suits a business that is launching a product range and needs the pack to match everything else from the first day, rather than a business that already has a settled look and only needs one pack laid out. If the launch includes photography, copywriting, structural design, or several pack formats for several products, the price will sit higher in this tier, and the quote should separate those parts so the business can see what it is paying for.

Agency or full launch: roughly $4,000 and above, as part of a full launch project. At this level the pack is one application of a full launch, designed by a team over weeks, with research, strategy, copywriting, photography, structural work and guidelines that govern every place the brand appears. The pack's share of that project is not a meaningful number on its own, because the project is buying the launch, not the pack. A small business rarely needs this tier for a pack alone, and it should not buy a full launch only to get a pack. It is included here so that the range is honest: the top of the market is not buying a pack, it is buying a launch, and the pack is one carefully governed part of it. If a business is quoted a four-figure price for a single simple label with no wider launch work, ask what the price covers. It may be justified by many variants, several formats, structural work, photography, or a complex finish, but it should be explained, not simply stated.

Materials, printing and finishing, for comparison, are separate general market ranges, and they are worth stating here so that the design ranges above are not mistaken for the total. As a general market range, a standard run of a few hundred simple packs on a standard material, with a standard finish, often costs from around one hundred dollars to several hundred dollars, depending on the quantity, the format, the material, the finish and the manufacturer. A run with a special material, a larger size, several variants, a foil or embossed finish, or a very small quantity costs more per pack, and a very large run costs less per pack than a small run of the same pack. Those production figures are broad, they vary by country and by manufacturer, and they are not a quote. The point of stating them is to show the shape of the total: for a standard pack, the design, the materials and the production are often of a similar order of magnitude, and none should be budgeted without the others, nor without the distribution. A business that budgets only for the design will be surprised by the production bill, and a business that budgets only for the production will be surprised by the design bill, and both will be surprised by the cost of getting the packs to the buyer. Budget for all of them, in writing, before the work starts.

What a Packaging Design Package Should Include

A proper packaging design package should be judged by its file list, not by its mockup. The mockup — the pack shown on a screen, on a shelf, or in a hand — is how the design is presented, and it is useful for judging the look. It is not the deliverable. The deliverable is the set of files that lets the pack be made correctly, shared correctly, and changed correctly in the future. At a minimum, the package should include print-ready artwork built on the final dieline, made to the manufacturer's or printer's specification, with the correct colour mode, resolution, bleed and safe margins, and with the barcode placed where it can be scanned. Bleed is the extra printed area beyond the pack's cut edge, usually a few millimetres on each side, which ensures that colour running to the edge is not left with a white hairline if the cutting shifts by a fraction. The safe margin is the distance inside the cut edge, the folds and the seams within which text and important details must sit, so that a tiny shift in cutting, folding or sealing does not clip a letter, a weight or a warning. The package should also include a visual mockup or rendered view of the finished pack, so that the business can check how the pack will look on a shelf before it is made. And it should state, in writing, how many design versions were offered, how many revision rounds are included, and what happens if a further round is needed.

Beyond the minimum, there are three items worth asking for, and worth paying for if they are not included. The first is the editable source file, or a clear statement of whether it is included and what it may be used for. The source file is the working file the designer built the pack in, and it is what another designer needs to change a flavour, a size, or a detail, or to adapt the pack to a new size, without rebuilding it. Some designers include it, some charge extra for it, and some retain it and offer small edits as a service. All three arrangements are legitimate, but the arrangement should be known before the work starts, because a pack without its source file is a pack that must be remade for every change. The second is a physical prototype or proof, checked against the dieline and the material, where the quantity or the complexity of the pack justifies it. A prototype is the only way to check, before production, that the pack folds, seals, opens and stands as intended, and that no headline lands on a fold and no text disappears into a seam. The third is a short written note of the pack's settings: the true size, the material, the print process, the finish, the bleed, the colour mode, the fonts used, and the manufacturer's specification the artwork was made to. That note costs the designer a few minutes and saves the next person — a different manufacturer, a different designer, or the business itself — from guessing. A package that includes the print-ready artwork, the mockup, the source-file arrangement in writing, and the settings note is a complete package. A package that includes only screen images, however handsome, is not a package at all. It is pictures of a pack.

The print-ready artwork also needs to be right in the technical details that a screen mockup hides. The colour mode matters: print artwork is usually prepared in CMYK, the four-ink process printers use, while screen files use RGB, the light-based mode screens use, and artwork prepared in the wrong mode can print with colours that look duller or shifted from the screen version. For some materials and finishes, a spot colour or a special ink may be specified instead, and the artwork must be prepared for the process the manufacturer will actually use. The resolution matters: photographs and any raster artwork in the pack should be at a high resolution at the pack's true size — commonly 300 dots per inch is quoted as the print standard — because a photograph that looks sharp on a phone screen can look soft or blocky when printed at pack size if it was supplied too small. The fonts matter: text should be embedded in the file or converted to outlines, so that the manufacturer's computer does not substitute a different font when the file is opened. The dieline matters most of all: the artwork must be built on the final dieline, with the folds, cuts, seams and glue areas in their final positions, and the designer should confirm the dieline with the manufacturer before delivery, because a pack built on an old dieline cannot be fixed after printing. And the barcode matters: it must be supplied in the right form, placed where it can be scanned, kept clear of folds and seams, and checked by scanning a proof, because a barcode that cannot be scanned will hold up the whole production run. A designer who checks all five — colour, resolution, fonts, dieline and barcode — before delivering the artwork is doing the part of the job that the client cannot see and the manufacturer will notice immediately. Ask the designer what checks they run before delivery, and ask the manufacturer to confirm the artwork when it arrives. A manufacturer who says the artwork is wrong is not being difficult. The manufacturer is the last person who can catch a problem before it is made a thousand times.

How to Brief a Designer for Packaging

The fastest way to get an accurate quote — and a good pack — is a brief that answers the questions the designer will otherwise have to guess. A good packaging brief is short, but it is complete. It states the one message the pack must carry, in one sentence, because a pack with one message can be designed and a pack with five messages cannot. It states the product, in one sentence: what it is, what size or weight it is, how it is used, and who it is for. It states the format — the label, box, pouch, tube or sleeve — or asks the designer and the manufacturer to recommend one, and it states the size range and the number of variants, because a range is priced as a system, not as a single pack. It lists, face by face, what each face must do, in one sentence per face, because the face plan is the pack: the front, the sides, and the back with its full details. It states the wording, or asks the designer to shape it, and says which it is, because wording shaped by the designer is a different job from wording supplied by the client. It lists every detail to be included, in full, with the weights and sizes checked, the barcode supplied in the right form, and the regulated information confirmed as complete and correct by the client or the client's adviser. It states the material, the finish, and the print process, or asks the designer and the manufacturer to recommend them, and it states the quantity to be made, or the quantity range, so that the designer and the manufacturer can both quote the real job. And it states how the pack will be stored, posted, displayed and opened, because the use decides the structure, the material and the reading situation. A brief that contains those answers will produce a quote that can be compared with another quote for the same job. A brief that omits them will produce quotes that cannot be compared, because each designer will have assumed a different pack. Mistakes That Cost Packaging Buyers Money

The mistakes that waste packaging money are usually small, and they are all avoidable. The first is choosing the format before the product: a size or shape decided by habit, rather than by writing down what each face must do, which produces a pack with faces that say nothing or faces that say too much. The second is designing the artwork before the dieline is final: artwork built on a draft template, which must be rebuilt when the manufacturer supplies the final dieline, and rebuilt again when the folds move. The third is supplying photographs too small, or in the wrong orientation, for the faces they must fill, which produces a pack that looks soft or badly cropped in exactly the places the buyer looks longest. The fourth is approving the design on a screen without checking a physical prototype, or at least a full-size print of the pack on the real material, which means the first production run is the proof, and any error in it is made a thousand times. The fifth is forgetting the folds and seams: a headline on a fold, a warning in a seam, or a barcode where it cannot be scanned, each of which is invisible on a screen and obvious in the hand. The sixth is budgeting for the design alone, and being surprised by the materials, production, finishing and distribution bills, which together are often as large as the design fee. The seventh is making a quantity chosen for the per-pack price rather than for the number that can actually be filled and sold before the content changes, which produces boxes of packs that are never used. And the eighth is treating the pack as finished when it is made, and never checking whether it is being noticed, picked up, or acted on — whether buyers mention it, whether it is taken from the shelf, or whether it is left behind — because a pack that is not working is a pack that should be changed, and a pack that is working is a pack worth reprinting. Check for these before anything else: is the face plan written down, is the dieline final and agreed with the manufacturer, are the words, barcode and photographs ready and in order, has a prototype been checked, has the manufacturer confirmed the artwork, and is the total — design, materials, production, finishing and distribution — known in writing? If the answer to all six is yes, the pack is ready to be designed. If the answer to any one is no, fix it first, because a pack that passes those six tests will be designed once and designed well, while a pack that fails any of them will be designed twice, or made twice, and paid for twice.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does packaging design cost in 2026?
As a general market range, a single custom label design typically runs from roughly seventy-five dollars to around three hundred dollars, a custom box or pouch design typically runs from roughly two hundred and fifty dollars to around twelve hundred dollars, and a full packaging system designed as part of a wider brand or launch package typically costs more, because it covers several formats, a consistent system and the wider launch work as well. The design fee is usually a one-off cost, separate from printing, materials, finishes and any moulds or plates, and the total depends on the format, the size range, the number of variants, whether the wording and photographs are supplied ready to use, the number of design versions, and the revision rounds included. Always get a written quote for your exact pack before budgeting from a range.

Is packaging design charged separately from printing?
Usually, yes. Design, printing, materials and finishing are different jobs, and they are usually charged separately. The design fee pays for the artwork: the layout on the dieline, the hierarchy of the information, the colours, the type and the print-ready files. The printing and production fee pays for the material, the ink or print process, the cutting and folding, and the number of packs ordered. Finishes such as foil, embossing, lamination or a special coating are usually charged as part of production. Some printers bundle a basic template layout with the print order, and some designers include a first print run in their package, so check every quote to see whether printing, materials and finishing are included or whether you will pay the printer separately.

What is a dieline, and why does it matter for cost?
A dieline is the flat template of a pack: the outline, the fold lines, the glue areas and the safe areas, drawn to the true size and shape of the finished pack. It matters for cost because the designer must lay the artwork out on that template, keeping text and important details clear of folds, cuts and glue areas, and because a pack without a correct dieline cannot be cut, folded and assembled as intended. Where the printer or manufacturer supplies the dieline, the design work starts from a known shape. Where no dieline exists — for a new shape, a new size, or a pack made by a new supplier — the dieline must be agreed with the manufacturer before the artwork is finished, and that step takes time and may add cost. Always confirm who supplies the dieline, and that the artwork is built on the final dieline, before approving the design.

What files should I receive for packaging?
You should receive print-ready artwork built on the final dieline, made to the printer's or manufacturer's specification, with the correct colour mode, resolution, bleed and safe margins, plus a visual mockup or rendered view of the finished pack for checking how it will look on a shelf. Ask for the pack size, the material, the print process, the finish, the barcode placement and the colour specification to be confirmed in writing before the files are made, because artwork made for one material, size or print process may not suit another. Keep the print-ready artwork, the mockup, and any editable source file that the package includes, so a future reprint or a small change does not mean paying for the whole design again.
