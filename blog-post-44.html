BROCHURE DESIGN PRICING

How Much Does Brochure Design Cost in 2026?

November 25, 2026 • 13 min read • By Muhammad Atif Iqbal

A brochure is the piece of print design that is asked to do the most work in the smallest space after a website: it must introduce a business, explain what it offers, show that it can be trusted, and leave the reader with one clear next step — and it must do all of that in a document the reader picked up voluntarily, will read in a few minutes, and may keep, pass on, or throw away. That combination is exactly why its price is so often misunderstood. From the outside, a brochure can look like a longer flyer: more pages, same idea. In practice, a good brochure is a piece of persuasion that has to work in order, page by page, with a cover that earns the opening, inside pages that build the case in the sequence the reader needs, and a final page that turns interest into action. The design fee pays for the judgement that makes that sequence work: what goes on the cover and what must wait, how much each page can say before it says too much, how photographs, headings and empty space guide the reader from one page to the next, and how the finished artwork must be prepared so that the printer returns the brochure the designer intended, with the pages in the right order, the folds in the right places, and nothing cut off or lost in the binding. This guide explains what brochure design cost actually covers in 2026, the general market ranges for design alone, how page counts, folds and binding move the price, what a proper brochure design package includes, and how design, printing, binding and distribution interact, so that the total cost of a finished, delivered brochure is clear before anyone is paid. One thing to be clear about up front: the ranges in this post are general market ranges, not quotes and not a promise of any price. Your exact price depends on your brochure, your pages, your format and binding, and your printer, and any designer worth hiring will quote your job in writing after reading your brief.

The reason the question matters is that a brochure is still, for many businesses, services and places, the document that is handed over at the moment of greatest interest: at an exhibition stand, in a showroom, at the end of a meeting, in a hotel lobby, or in the post after an enquiry. A website waits to be found, and a social media post waits to be shown, but a brochure is placed in the reader's hands at the moment they have already shown interest, and it is the document they take away to read properly, to show to someone else, or to keep until they are ready to decide. That role is what makes the design stage worth doing properly, even though the brochure may be only a few pages. If the cover is unclear, if the order of the pages confuses the reader, if the prices or the contact details are hard to find, or if the brochure looks crowded and cheap beside the brochures it will sit next to, the business lives with that impression for the whole print run, because a printed brochure cannot be edited after it leaves the printer. That permanence, combined with the short time a brochure gets to make its point on each page, is what makes the cheapest route risky: a brochure assembled in a hurry from a generic template, with no check of the page order or the binding, can look acceptable on a screen and disappointing in the hand, because a screen hides the very things print reveals — small text that cannot be read comfortably, a photograph that breaks up when printed large, a fold that lands across a headline, and a centre spread that disappears into the binding. The useful question is therefore not "what is the cheapest brochure?" but "what should I pay for a brochure design that will be opened, read in order, kept, and acted on, and printed and bound well at the quantity I need?" The rest of this guide answers that question, starting with what brochure design actually is, and what it is not.

What Brochure Design Actually Is

Brochure design is the layout of a multi-page printed document so that a reader can understand it in order, remember the one thing it is asking them to do, and know how to do it. The designer decides the sequence: which piece of information is met first, which second, and which can wait until the reader is ready for it. In most cases the cover comes first, because it is what earns the opening: the business, the subject, and the promise, stated in a few words and one strong image that can be understood at a glance. The inside pages come next, in the order the reader needs them: the problem or the opportunity, the offer, the evidence, the detail, and the reassurance, each given the space it needs and no more. The final page carries the next step — call, book, visit, order, or enquire — set large enough to act on without hunting, with the contact details, the address, and anything the reader needs to take the step. A designer who understands sequence will resist the temptation to fill every page. Empty space in a brochure is not wasted space. It is what lets the eye find the heading, and it is what keeps the brochure from looking like a website that has been printed until it is unreadable. A brochure that tries to say everything on every page will be read by nobody, because the reader will conclude, in the first few seconds, that reading it would take too long, and will put it down.

What brochure design is not is a new brand identity, and it is not a website. A brochure design normally works with artwork and colours that already exist: the business name, its existing mark, its colours and its fonts, supplied by the client or taken from an earlier brand package. If the business does not yet have those things, the brochure job becomes larger, because the designer must first settle the colours, the fonts and the arrangement that the brochure will then use — and that wider work is priced separately, as our companion post on how much a logo costs in 2026 explains for the identity side of the work. A brochure is also not a website in miniature. A website can carry pages of detail, because a visitor who has arrived at a website has already decided to look, and can search within it. A brochure must carry its message in a fixed order, because its reader meets the pages in the order they are bound, and cannot search. That distinction matters when comparing quotes and when writing the brief. One quote may assume the client will supply the artwork, the wording and the photographs, ready to lay out, while another may include time to tidy the artwork, choose matching fonts, source or improve photographs, or shorten and shape the wording so that it fits the pages. Both can be fair quotes, but they are quotes for different jobs, and the cheaper one is not cheaper if the wording still needs to be written or the photographs still need to be found before the brochure can be laid out. Ask, before you accept any quote, what the designer expects you to supply, and what the designer will prepare. The answer will tell you whether the price covers the brochure alone, or the brochure plus the preparation that makes the brochure possible.

Brochure, Flyer, Leaflet, Catalogue: Knowing Which One You Need

The words brochure, flyer, leaflet and catalogue are often used as if they meant the same thing, and the confusion costs money, because each one is a different job with a different price. A flyer is a single, usually unfolded sheet, printed on one or both sides, meant to be handed out, posted, or left in a stack for people to take, carrying one message and one next step, read in seconds — the subject of our companion post on how much flyer design costs in 2026. A leaflet is a folded sheet, usually with several panels, meant to be read in order, like a small booklet, carrying more information than a flyer because the reader who opens it has already decided to read further. A brochure is a multi-page document, usually bound or stapled, that a reader keeps: it carries the full case for a business, a service, a place or a product range, with room for photographs, detail, evidence and prices, read in order over several minutes. A catalogue is larger still: a listing of many products or services, with prices and specifications, meant to be consulted repeatedly rather than read through once. The four are priced differently because they are designed differently. A flyer is one or two layouts. A leaflet is several panels that must work in order and fold correctly. A brochure is several pages that must work as a sequence, with a cover, inside pages and a closing page, and with binding that must be planned from the start. A catalogue is many pages built to a repeated system, where consistency across the whole document matters more than any single page. Before asking for a quote, decide which of the four you need, by asking how the reader will meet it and how long they will keep it. If it will be handed out and read in seconds, it is a flyer. If it will be picked up and read in order, with several things to explain, it is a leaflet. If it will be kept, read properly, shown to someone else, and used to make a decision, it is a brochure. If it will be consulted repeatedly as a reference for many items, it is a catalogue. A designer who is told the wrong format will design a document that looks striking and fails in use: a brochure crammed onto a flyer that cannot be read, or a catalogue laid out as if it were a brochure and impossible to consult. The format should follow the reading situation, not fashion, and the quote should follow the format.

Page Counts and Formats: 4 Pages, 6 Panels, 8 to 16 Pages, and Booklets

The most common brochure formats are the four-page brochure, the six-panel folded brochure, and the booklet of eight, twelve or sixteen pages, and each suits a different job. A four-page brochure — a single sheet folded once, making a cover, two inside pages and a back page — is the shortest true brochure: large enough to carry an introduction, the main offer, the evidence, and the next step, and small enough to be handed out, posted, or left in a rack without seeming like a commitment to read. It is the safe, practical choice for a business that needs to say more than a flyer can say, without asking the reader to read a booklet. A six-panel folded brochure — a sheet folded twice, making six panels — suits a business that needs to explain a service, a menu, a set of prices, or a sequence of steps in the order the reader will open the panels. Its front panel is the cover, its inside panels carry the detail in order, and its back panel carries the contact details and the next step. A booklet of eight, twelve or sixteen pages suits a business, a place, or a product range that needs room: several services, several rooms, several destinations, or several case studies, each given its own page or spread, with photographs large enough to be enjoyed rather than merely identified. Booklets are usually built in multiples of four pages, because of the way printed sheets fold and bind, and a booklet that is planned at ten pages will usually be made as twelve, with the extra pages used well or left deliberately quiet, rather than squeezed into a format the printer cannot bind. Before choosing a format, write down what each page, or each panel, must do in one sentence. If you cannot write that sentence for a page, the brochure probably does not need that page. If you can write it for every page, the page count is right, and the quote can be written for the real job. Print one copy at the intended size, hold it at the distance at which it will be read, and check that the cover is inviting, the order is clear, and the final page is findable. That test, done once, will settle the format better than any discussion on a screen.

Page count also affects the design work, and therefore the price, in a way that is easy to overlook. A longer brochure is not simply a shorter brochure with more pages added. Every additional page is a layout to design, balance and proof, every additional spread must work with the pages beside it, and a longer brochure usually needs more wording, more photographs, and more checking of the order in which a reader meets the information. A photograph that looks sharp on one page may look soft when used larger on another, because it is being printed larger from the same file, and the designer must check the resolution at the true size on every page before the layout is approved. A heading set for a four-page brochure will need to be reset for a booklet, because the hierarchy across many pages must be consistent, so the reader always knows where they are. A brochure that must work in two sizes — a printed booklet and a screen version for the website, for example — is two layouts, not one, even when the wording is the same, because the shape, the hierarchy and the image crops must all be reworked for the second size. That second version is often worth paying for, because a brochure that is designed once and shared everywhere will usually look wrong somewhere: cropped, cramped, or unreadable at the size where it is actually seen. Ask, before the work starts, whether the quote covers one size or several, and whether a digital version for sharing is included. A quote that covers one size and leaves the digital version to be made later, by someone else, from screenshots of the print file, will produce a digital brochure that looks like photographs of a brochure, which is what it will be. A package that includes the print file and a properly made screen version, each designed for its own size, is a fuller package, and it is worth comparing quotes on that basis, page by page.

Folds and Binding: What Holds the Brochure Together

How a brochure is folded or bound decides how it is read, and it must be decided before the artwork is finished, because the fold and the binding decide where a page begins and ends, where a photograph may be lost, and where the reader pauses. A four-page brochure is usually folded once, and the fold is its centre: the two inside pages meet at the fold, and a spread that runs across the fold must be designed with the fold in mind, so that no headline, face or important detail is lost in it. A six-panel brochure is folded twice, and each fold changes how the reader meets the information: the front panel is the cover, the inside panels are read in the order they open, and the back panel is seen last. A fold that lands across a headline, a photograph, or a line of text will break the reading, and a panel that is read out of order will confuse the reader, so the designer must plan the fold from the start, with the printer's fold specification agreed before the artwork is finished. A booklet is usually saddle-stitched — stapled through the fold — for shorter booklets, or bound with a spine for thicker ones, and the binding decides the centre most of all: a booklet opened flat will lose a small amount of each centre spread into the spine, and text, faces and important details must sit clear of it. A thicker booklet may need a spine of its own, with the title on it, and the cover artwork must be planned for the spine width the printer specifies. Binding also decides the paper: a booklet on a heavier paper will be thicker, will need a different binding, and may crack at the fold if the paper and the binding are not chosen together, so the fold, the binding and the paper must be chosen together, and the printer should advise on the combination. Tell the designer and the printer how the brochure will be used, before the binding is chosen, because the use decides the binding, and the binding decides part of the price. A brochure that will be posted should be light and flat. A brochure that will be kept on a shelf should feel substantial. A brochure that will be handed out at an event should open easily in one hand. Each use points to a different fold, a different binding, and a different price, and the choice should be made deliberately, once, before the design begins.

Copy and Photography: The Work Before the Layout

A brochure is only as good as the words and photographs it is given to lay out, and the work of preparing them is often the largest hidden part of a brochure job. A flyer needs a headline and a few details. A brochure needs a cover line, an introduction, a page or a spread for each part of the case, captions for the photographs, and a closing page with the next step — and each of those must be written for the reader who will meet it in that order, not copied from a website in the order a website presents it. One quote may assume the client will supply the wording, ready to lay out, while another may include time to write, shorten and shape the wording so that it fits the pages and reads in order. Both can be fair quotes, but they are quotes for different jobs, and the cheaper one is not cheaper if the wording still needs to be written before the brochure can be laid out. The same is true of photographs. A brochure needs more photographs than a flyer, at larger sizes, and each photograph must be sharp at the size it will be printed at, in the orientation the layout needs. A photograph that looks sharp on a phone screen can look soft or blocky when printed large on a brochure page if it was supplied too small, and a photograph supplied in the wrong orientation will either be cropped badly or leave the layout with a gap it cannot fill. A designer who must source, improve or retouch photographs, or commission new ones, is doing real work that belongs in the quote, and a client who supplies photographs should supply them at the largest size available, in the original files, so the designer can choose the crop that suits each page. Where photography must be commissioned, it should be briefed as part of the brochure job, with the pages it must fill in mind, so the photographer shoots the subjects, the orientations and the empty space the layout will need. The cheapest brochure is rarely the one with the cheapest layout. It is the one whose words and photographs were ready, in the right order, before the layout began, so the designer's time was spent designing rather than waiting, chasing, or rewriting.

Design Cost Versus Printing, Binding and Distribution: Four Separate Bills

The most common confusion in brochure pricing is the belief that there is one price for a brochure. In practice there are almost always four: the price of designing the brochure, the price of printing it, the price of binding or finishing it, and the price of getting it to the reader. The design fee is usually a one-off payment. It pays for the designer's time: understanding the brief, planning the sequence, arranging the content page by page, choosing and setting the type, placing the artwork and the photographs, preparing the print-ready files, and making the agreed revision rounds. Once the design is approved and the files are delivered, the design fee is done, and a reprint of the same brochure should not need a new design fee, unless the content changes. The printing fee is a per-run payment. It pays for the paper, the ink, and the labour of producing the number of brochures ordered, and it is paid again every time a new batch is printed. Binding and finishing are usually charged as part of the print production: the folding, the stapling or binding, the trimming, and any special finish, each of which adds a step and a cost that grows with the page count and the quantity. The distribution cost is the fourth bill, and it is the one most often forgotten: the postage for a mailing, the cost of a delivery service, the time spent handing brochures out or placing them in racks and stands, and the cost of the envelopes or the holders they will sit in. Some printers bundle a basic layout service with the print order, usually from a template library, and some designers include a first print run in their package price. Both arrangements are legitimate, but they must be read carefully. A bundled layout is not the same as a custom design, and a package that includes printing is only good value if the paper, finish, binding and quantity it includes are the ones the business actually wants. Always ask, in plain words: does this price include the design, the printing, the binding and finishing, the distribution, or all four, and if it includes printing, how many brochures, on what paper, with what binding and what finish?

Separating the four bills also makes the long-term cost clearer. A custom design that costs more up front can be the cheaper choice over several print runs, because the design is paid for once and the reprints cost only the printing, binding and distribution. A cheap template layout that is tied to one printer, in one file format that cannot be taken elsewhere, can become expensive when the business changes printer, changes a price or a date, or wants the same brochure in a different size, because the layout must then be remade. Distribution deserves the same care. A thousand brochures that sit in a box because nobody was paid or given time to deliver them are not cheap brochures. They are wasted brochures, and the money spent printing them would have been better spent on a smaller run, properly delivered, and a second, updated run later. When you order the first run, ask how a reprint will work, what file will be used, and whether a small change — a new date, a new price, a new phone number — will be charged as a small edit or as a new design. The answer is part of the true cost of the brochure, even though it will not be paid until later. And when you compare quotes, compare design with design, printing with printing, binding with binding, and distribution with distribution. A quote that looks expensive because it includes a thousand printed and bound brochures is not comparable with a quote that prices the design alone. Put the four parts side by side, in the same table, with the same quantity, the same paper, the same binding and the same finish, and the comparison will be honest. If a quote does not separate the four parts, ask for the separation. A designer or printer who will not separate them is asking you to buy without knowing what you are buying. Our companion post on how much business card design costs in 2026 covers the smaller, permanent card that often accompanies a brochure, and the two should be budgeted together where both are needed, so that the card and the brochure match and the total is known before either is ordered.

How Quantity Changes the Total

Printing quantity changes the total in a way that surprises many first-time buyers, because the relationship is not a straight line, and because binding makes the curve steeper for brochures than for single sheets. The design fee does not change with quantity: designing a brochure that will be printed one hundred times costs the same as designing a brochure that will be printed five thousand times, because the layout is the same. The printing and binding fees, however, fall sharply per brochure as the quantity rises, because the fixed costs of setting up the print job — preparing the file, setting the paper, checking the first sheets, setting the folder and the binder — are spread across more brochures. The first hundred brochures therefore carry a high cost per brochure, the next few hundred carry a much lower one, and by the time the run reaches a few thousand, the cost per brochure is often a small fraction of what it was for the first hundred. That is why printers quote in bands, and why the jump from one band to the next can look like very good value. It is good value only if the brochures will be used. A thousand brochures that sit in a drawer because the prices changed, the offer ended, or the business moved are not cheap brochures. They are wasted brochures, and the money spent printing them would have been better spent on a smaller run and a second, updated run later. The sensible quantity is therefore a judgement, not a calculation. A brochure for a one-off event, with a fixed date, should be printed in the quantity that can be distributed before the event, with a small margin for the brochures that will be lost or damaged, and no more. A brochure for an ongoing service, with no date on it, can justify a larger run, because the per-brochure saving is real and the brochures will be used before anything changes. A brochure with prices on it should be printed in the quantity that can be used before the prices are likely to change, or the prices should be kept on a separate insert that can be replaced without reprinting the whole brochure — a practical arrangement worth discussing with the designer and the printer before the layout is finished. Reprints also deserve a line in the budget. A brochure design that is kept as a proper print-ready file, with the editable source file held by the business or the designer, can be reprinted by any competent printer, with only the printing and binding to pay for. A brochure that exists only as a low-resolution image, or only in a printer's online template system, cannot be reprinted elsewhere without being remade, and the remake will cost design time again. Get the printing and binding quote in writing, for the exact quantity, paper, finish, page count and binding, before approving the design. A design approved for a binding or a finish the budget cannot afford will have to be redesigned, and that redesign is the most avoidable cost in the whole brochure project.

General Market Ranges for Brochure Design

The ranges below are what you will typically see across online print services, freelance marketplaces, designer portfolios and studio pricing pages, for the design work on a brochure. They are general market ranges, not quotes, and they do not include printing, binding or distribution unless the text says so. The exact price for your brochure depends on the page count, the format and binding, whether the wording and photographs are supplied ready to use, how many design versions and revision rounds are included, and whether a digital version for sharing is also needed. Treat these as a map, not a price list, and always get a written quote for your exact brochure.

Template or online print-service layout: roughly $0 to around $75, often bundled with printing. You choose a template, type your details into it, and the printer prints the result. The design work is selection and typing, not custom layout, and the result can be perfectly serviceable for a straightforward brochure with a single message and standard details: a short folded brochure for an event, a service, or a notice. The trade-offs are the ones that apply to any template: the layout is shared with other businesses, the fonts and spacing cannot be adjusted far, the photograph choices are limited, the page count is fixed by the template, and the file usually stays inside the printer's system, so a reprint elsewhere means starting again. This tier suits a brochure that is needed urgently, a brochure for a short-lived purpose, or a business that wants to test its wording before paying for a custom design. It does not suit a brochure that must look distinct, carry a booklet format, use photographs that need careful cropping, or match an existing brand precisely.

Custom brochure design by a freelancer: roughly $200 to $900 for a short brochure. A designer lays out the brochure for this business alone, working from the supplied artwork, wording and photographs, with one or two design versions and one or two revision rounds, and delivers print-ready files. This is the tier where most small-business brochures land, and it is usually the best value for a standard brochure: the layout is original, the cover is set for the brochure's real size and reading situation, the pages work in order, the files are prepared for the printer and the binding, and the brochure can be reprinted from those files. The range is wide because a four-page brochure with supplied artwork and one revision round is a smaller job than an eight- or twelve-page booklet with photographs to prepare, a price list to set, several versions for several locations, and several revision rounds. A brochure that needs the wording shortened and shaped, the photographs improved, or a digital version for sharing will sit at the higher end of this tier, and that is fair, because each of those is real work. A booklet beyond about twelve pages, or a brochure with complex tables, maps, or many product entries, will usually sit above this tier, because the page-by-page work grows with every page. Check the portfolio for printed brochures, not only screen mockups, and ask whether the price includes the print-ready files, the digital version, and the revision rounds you expect.

Brochure design within a campaign or studio package: roughly $900 to $3,000, usually as part of wider work. A studio rarely prices a single brochure in isolation at this tier. The brochure is designed as part of a campaign — the social media images, the flyer, the poster, the email header, the exhibition display — with a set process, more than one person checking the work, and a consistent look across every piece. The brochure itself may be one item in a package that costs several hundred dollars or more, and judging it by the brochure alone will make it look expensive. Judged as part of the campaign, it is the cost of consistency: the same headline, colours and images on the brochure, the flyer and the social media posts, checked by a team. This tier suits a business that is launching a service, a place or a product range and needs the brochure to match everything else from the first day, rather than a business that already has a settled look and only needs one brochure laid out. If the campaign includes photography, copywriting, or several brochure versions for several audiences, the price will sit higher in this tier, and the quote should separate those parts so the business can see what it is paying for.

Agency or full campaign: roughly $3,000 and above, as part of a full campaign project. At this level the brochure is one application of a full campaign, designed by a team over weeks, with research, strategy, copywriting, photography and guidelines that govern every place the campaign appears. The brochure's share of that project is not a meaningful number on its own, because the project is buying the campaign, not the brochure. A small business rarely needs this tier for a brochure alone, and it should not buy a full campaign only to get a brochure. It is included here so that the range is honest: the top of the market is not buying a brochure, it is buying a campaign, and the brochure is one carefully governed part of it. If a business is quoted a four-figure price for a single short brochure with no wider campaign work, ask what the price covers. It may be justified by many pages, several versions, photography, or a complex binding, but it should be explained, not simply stated.

Printing and binding, for comparison, are separate general market ranges, and they are worth stating here so that the design ranges above are not mistaken for the total. As a general market range, a standard run of a few hundred short brochures on a standard paper, with a standard fold or staple binding, often costs from around one hundred dollars to several hundred dollars, depending on the quantity, the page count, the paper, the binding and the printer. A run with a thicker paper, a larger page count, a bound spine, or a very small quantity costs more per brochure, and a very large run costs less per brochure than a small run of the same brochure. Those printing figures are broad, they vary by country and by printer, and they are not a quote. The point of stating them is to show the shape of the total: for a standard brochure, the design, the printing and the binding are often of a similar order of magnitude, and none should be budgeted without the others, nor without the distribution. A business that budgets only for the design will be surprised by the printing and binding bill, and a business that budgets only for the printing will be surprised by the design bill, and both will be surprised by the cost of getting the brochures to the reader. Budget for all four, in writing, before the work starts.

What a Brochure Design Package Should Include

A proper brochure design package should be judged by its file list, not by its mockup. The mockup — the brochure shown on a screen, on a desk, or in a hand — is how the design is presented, and it is useful for judging the look. It is not the deliverable. The deliverable is the set of files that lets the brochure be printed and bound correctly, shared correctly, and changed correctly in the future. At a minimum, the package should include a print-ready PDF of the whole brochure, made to the printer's specification, with the pages in the correct order for printing and binding, with bleed, with trim marks if the printer asks for them, and in the colour mode the printer requires. Bleed is the extra printed area beyond the brochure's cut edge, usually a few millimetres on each side, which ensures that colour running to the edge is not left with a white hairline if the cutting shifts by a fraction. The pages must be in the order the printer needs — which, for a booklet, is not always the order the reader reads them in — and the designer, not the client, should be the one who arranges them, because a booklet imposed in the wrong order will be printed with the pages in the wrong places. The package should also include a screen version of the brochure — usually a PDF or a set of images — for email, messaging, and the website, so that the brochure can be shared without sending the print file. And it should state, in writing, how many design versions were offered, how many revision rounds are included, and what happens if a further round is needed.

Beyond the minimum, there are three items worth asking for, and worth paying for if they are not included. The first is the editable source file, or a clear statement of whether it is included and what it may be used for. The source file is the working file the designer built the brochure in, and it is what another designer needs to change a date, a price, or a phone number, or to adapt the brochure to a new size, without rebuilding it. Some designers include it, some charge extra for it, and some retain it and offer small edits as a service. All three arrangements are legitimate, but the arrangement should be known before the work starts, because a brochure without its source file is a brochure that must be remade for every change. The second is the binding and fold artwork, checked against the printer's specification: the layout checked for the fold positions and the spine, with the centre spread kept clear of the binding, so that no headline lands on a fold and no text disappears into the spine. The third is a short written note of the brochure's settings: the true page size, the page count, the bleed, the binding method, the colour mode, the fonts used, and the printer's specification the file was made to. That note costs the designer a few minutes and saves the next person — a different printer, a different designer, or the business itself — from guessing. A package that includes the print-ready file, the screen version, the source-file arrangement in writing, and the settings note is a complete package. A package that includes only screen images, however handsome, is not a package at all. It is pictures of a brochure.

The print-ready file also needs to be right in the technical details that a screen mockup hides. The colour mode matters: print files are usually prepared in CMYK, the four-ink process printers use, while screen files use RGB, the light-based mode screens use, and a file prepared in the wrong mode can print with colours that look duller or shifted from the screen version. The resolution matters: photographs and any raster artwork in the brochure should be at a high resolution at the brochure's true size — commonly 300 dots per inch is quoted as the print standard — because a photograph that looks sharp on a phone screen can look soft or blocky when printed at brochure size if it was supplied too small. The fonts matter: text should be embedded in the PDF or converted to outlines, so that the printer's computer does not substitute a different font when the file is opened. The page order matters most of all for a booklet: the pages must be imposed in the order the printer and binder need, and the designer should confirm the imposition with the printer before delivery, because a booklet with its pages in the wrong order cannot be fixed after printing. And the safe margin matters: text and important details should sit a few millimetres inside the cut edge and away from any fold or the spine, so that a tiny shift in cutting, folding or binding does not clip a letter, a price or a phone number. A designer who checks all five — colour mode, resolution, fonts, page order and safe margin — before delivering the file is doing the part of the job that the client cannot see and the printer will notice immediately. Ask the designer what checks they run before delivery, and ask the printer to confirm the file when it arrives. A printer who says the file is wrong is not being difficult. The printer is the last person who can catch a problem before it is printed a thousand times.

How to Brief a Designer for a Brochure

The fastest way to get an accurate quote — and a good brochure — is a brief that answers the questions the designer will otherwise have to guess. A good brochure brief is short, but it is complete. It states the one message the brochure must carry, in one sentence, because a brochure with one message can be designed and a brochure with five messages cannot. It states the audience, in one sentence, because a brochure for new customers, for existing customers, or for a trade audience is a different document in each case. It lists, page by page, what each page or spread must do, in one sentence per page, because the page plan is the brochure: the cover, the introduction, each part of the case, and the closing page with the next step. It states the headline, or asks the designer to write one, and says which it is, because a headline written by the designer is a different job from a headline supplied by the client. It lists every detail to be included, in full, with the prices checked, the address checked to confirm that it is the address the reader should go to, the phone number in the format it should be dialled, and the website address checked to confirm that it loads. It states the offer or the prices, if there are any, exactly as they should appear, including any terms that must be shown. It states the format — the page size, the page count, the fold or binding — or asks the designer to recommend one, and it states the quantity to be printed, or the quantity range, so that the designer and the printer can both quote the real job. And it states how the brochure will be distributed — handed out, posted, racked, or kept in a showroom — because the distribution decides the size, the paper, the binding, and the reading situation. A brief that contains those answers will produce a quote that can be compared with another quote for the same job. A brief that omits them will produce quotes that cannot be compared, because each designer will have assumed a different brochure. Mistakes That Cost Brochure Buyers Money

The mistakes that waste brochure money are usually small, and they are all avoidable. The first is choosing the format before the content: a page count decided by habit, rather than by writing down what each page must do, which produces a brochure with pages that say nothing or pages that say too much. The second is copying the wording from a website in website order, rather than shaping it for the order a reader meets in print, which produces a brochure that reads like a website that has been printed. The third is supplying photographs too small, or in the wrong orientation, for the pages they must fill, which produces a brochure that looks soft or badly cropped in exactly the places the reader looks longest. The fourth is approving the design on a screen without checking a printed proof, or at least a full-size print of the cover and one inside spread, which means the first printed copy is the proof, and any error in it is printed a thousand times. The fifth is forgetting the binding: a centre spread designed across the spine, a headline on a fold, or text too close to the edge, each of which is invisible on a screen and obvious in the hand. The sixth is budgeting for the design alone, and being surprised by the printing, binding and distribution bills, which together are often as large as the design fee. The seventh is printing a quantity chosen for the per-brochure price rather than for the number that can actually be distributed before the content changes, which produces boxes of brochures that are never used. And the eighth is treating the brochure as finished when it is printed, and never checking whether it is being read, kept, or acted on — whether enquiries mention it, whether it is taken from the rack, or whether it is left behind at events — because a brochure that is not working is a brochure that should be changed, and a brochure that is working is a brochure worth reprinting. Check for these before anything else: is the page plan written down, are the words and photographs ready and in order, has a printed proof been checked, has the printer confirmed the file and the binding, and is the total — design, printing, binding and distribution — known in writing? If the answer to all five is yes, the brochure is ready to be designed. If the answer to any one is no, fix it first, because a brochure that passes those five tests will be designed once and designed well, while a brochure that fails any of them will be designed twice, or printed twice, and paid for twice.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does brochure design cost in 2026?
As a general market range, a template-based brochure layout typically costs very little or nothing beyond printing, a custom short brochure from a freelancer typically runs from roughly two hundred dollars to around nine hundred dollars, and a longer booklet brochure designed as part of a wider studio or campaign package typically costs more because the price covers more pages, more content work and the wider campaign as well. The design fee is usually a one-off cost, separate from printing and binding, and the total depends on the page count, the format and binding, whether the wording and photographs are supplied ready to use, the number of design versions, and the revision rounds included. Always get a written quote for your exact brochure before budgeting from a range.

Is brochure design charged separately from printing and binding?
Usually, yes. Design, printing and binding are three different jobs, and they are usually charged separately. The design fee pays for the layout of every page, the cover, the arrangement of the message, and the print-ready files. The printing fee pays for the paper, the ink and the production of the number of brochures ordered. Binding or folding is a finishing step — stapling a booklet, folding a leaflet-style brochure, or trimming and binding a thicker one — and it is usually charged as part of the print production. Some printers bundle a basic template layout with the print order, and some designers include a first print run in their package, so check every quote to see whether printing and finishing are included or whether you will pay the printer separately.

Does a longer brochure cost more than a short one?
Yes, usually, and page count is the single largest driver of brochure design cost. Every additional page is a layout to design, balance and proof, every additional spread must work with the pages beside it, and a longer brochure usually needs more wording, more photographs, and more checking of the order in which a reader meets the information. Printing and binding also usually cost more as pages are added, because more paper is used and a thicker brochure may need a different binding. The difference between a four-page brochure and a sixteen-page booklet is therefore not a small step. It is a different job, and it should be quoted as one.

What files should I receive for a brochure?
You should receive a print-ready PDF of the whole brochure, made to the printer's specification, with the pages in the correct order for printing and binding, with bleed, trim marks and the correct colour mode for the printer you will use, plus a screen version for email, messaging or the website. Ask for the page size, the page count, the bleed allowance, the binding method, the resolution and the colour mode to be confirmed in writing before the files are made, because a file made for one printer, one binding or one size may not suit another. Keep the print-ready file, the screen version, and any editable source file that the package includes, so a future reprint or a small change does not mean paying for the whole design again.
