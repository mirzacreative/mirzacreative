PITCH DECK DESIGN PRICING

How Much Does Pitch Deck Design Cost in 2026?

November 27, 2026 • 14 min read • By Muhammad Atif Iqbal

A pitch deck is the only piece of design that must persuade a live audience, in a fixed order, in a limited time, while being spoken over. A logo, a business card or a flyer can be judged on a screen, at the reader's own pace. A deck cannot. It is judged in a room, slide by slide, by people who are listening as well as looking, who will remember the argument in the order it was presented, and who will ask questions the moment the presentation ends. That combination is exactly why its price is so often misunderstood. From the outside, a deck can look like a set of slides: a title, some bullet points, a chart or two. In practice, a good deck is a piece of persuasion that has to work in three ways at once: the slides must carry the argument in order, the visuals must make the evidence plain at a glance, and the speaker must be able to present it without reading it. This guide explains what pitch deck design cost actually covers in 2026, the general market ranges for design alone, how slide count, story work, data visuals and deadlines move the price, what a proper pitch deck package includes, and how design, content, presentation practice and follow-up material interact, so that the total cost of a finished, presentable deck is clear before anyone is paid. One thing to be clear about up front: the ranges in this post are general market ranges, not quotes and not a promise of any price. Your exact price depends on your deck, your slide count, your story and content, your charts and visuals, and your deadline, and any designer worth hiring will quote your job in writing after reading your brief and seeing your material.

The reason the question matters is that a pitch deck is, for many businesses, the document that decides whether a conversation continues. A website waits to be found, and a brochure waits to be read, but a deck is presented to people who have already agreed to listen, and it is the thing they judge, remember, and discuss after the presenter has left the room. That role is what makes the design stage worth doing properly, even though the deck may be short. If the story is out of order, if the evidence is hard to read, if the slides are crowded with text the audience tries to read instead of listening, or if the deck looks like a template the audience has seen before, the business lives with that impression for the whole meeting, because a presentation cannot be edited once it has been given. That permanence, combined with the short time a deck gets to make each point, is what makes the cheapest route risky: a deck assembled in a hurry from a generic template, with no check of the story's order, the charts' honesty, or the text's size at presentation distance, can look acceptable on a laptop screen and disappointing in a room, because a laptop hides the very things a live audience reveals — text too small to read from the back, a chart whose meaning takes a minute to grasp, a slide that tries to say three things at once, and a speaker who must read the slides because the slides were written to be read. The useful question is therefore not "what is the cheapest deck?" but "what should I pay for a deck that will be understood in order, believed, remembered, and acted on, and presented well on the day?" The rest of this guide answers that question, starting with what pitch deck design actually is, and what it is not.

What Pitch Deck Design Actually Is

Pitch deck design is the design of a sequence of slides so that an audience can follow an argument in order, understand the evidence at a glance, and remember the point when the presentation ends. The designer decides the order: which point is met first, which second, and which can wait until the audience is ready for it. In most cases the problem comes first, because it is what earns attention: the problem stated plainly, in the audience's own words, with evidence that it is real and worth solving. The solution comes next, in the order the audience needs it: what the product or service does, how it works, and why it is different from what already exists. The evidence comes after the claim, in the order the audience will test it: the market, the model, the traction, and the plan, each given the space it needs and no more. A designer who understands order will resist the temptation to put everything on the first slides. Empty space on a slide is not wasted space. It is what lets the audience find the one point the slide exists to make, and it is what keeps the audience listening to the speaker instead of reading the slide. A slide that tries to say everything will be read by nobody, because the audience will conclude, in the first few seconds, that reading it would take too long, and will stop listening while they try.

What pitch deck design is not is a set of slides decorated to look impressive, and it is not a document shrunk onto slides. A deck normally works with material that already exists: the business idea, its figures, its story, and its brand, supplied by the client or taken from an earlier brand package. If the business does not yet have those things settled — if the story has not been agreed, if the figures have not been checked, or if the brand's colours and fonts are not decided — the deck job becomes larger, because the designer must first help settle the story, the order, and the look that the deck will then use, and that wider work is priced separately, as our companion post on how much a logo costs in 2026 explains for the identity side of the work. A deck is also not a report laid out as slides. A report is read at the reader's pace, can be re-read, and can carry detail. A deck is presented at the speaker's pace, cannot be re-read during the meeting, and must carry only what the audience needs to grasp while listening. That distinction matters when comparing quotes and when writing the brief. One quote may assume the client will supply the story, the wording, and the figures, ready to lay out, and that the slide count is fixed, while another may include time to shape the story, shorten the wording so that it fits the slides, and build the charts from raw figures. Both can be fair quotes, but they are quotes for different jobs, and the cheaper one is not cheaper if the story still needs to be written, the figures still need to be checked, or the slide count still needs to be decided before the deck can be laid out. Ask, before you accept any quote, what the designer expects you to supply, and what the designer will prepare. The answer will tell you whether the price covers the slide design alone, or the slide design plus the story work that makes the deck persuasive.

Investor Decks, Sales Decks and Internal Decks: Knowing Which Deck You Need

The word pitch deck covers several different kinds of deck, and the confusion costs money, because each one is a different job with a different price. An investor deck is a deck presented to people who may fund the business. It must persuade strangers who know nothing about the business that the problem is real, the solution is credible, the market is worth serving, and the team can deliver the plan. A sales deck is a deck presented to people who may buy the product or service. It must persuade a buyer that their problem is understood, that the offer solves it, and that buying is the sensible next step. An internal deck is a deck presented to colleagues, to a board, or to a team. It must inform people who already know the subject, in an order they can follow, so that a decision can be made. A conference or keynote deck is a deck presented to a large audience, often with little time per slide, that must entertain and inform at once, and be understood from the back of a large room. The four are priced differently because they are designed differently. An investor deck needs its story built with care, its evidence checked, and its financial slides built from figures the presenter can defend. A sales deck needs its argument built around the buyer's problem, with proof the buyer will trust and a clear next step. An internal deck needs clarity and honesty above polish, because its audience will ask hard questions. A keynote deck needs large type, strong visuals, and very little text, because its audience reads from a distance and listens at the same time. Before asking for a quote, decide which deck you need, by asking who will be in the room, what decision they will make, and how much time you will have. If the audience must be persuaded to fund or to buy, it is a persuasion deck. If the audience must be informed so that a decision can be made, it is an information deck. If the audience is large and distant, it is a keynote deck. A designer who is told the wrong kind will design a deck that looks striking and fails in the room: an investor deck that reads like a sales brochure, a sales deck that reads like a financial report, or a keynote deck whose text cannot be read from the back. The kind should follow the audience, the decision, and the room, not fashion, and the quote should follow the kind.

Story, Structure and Slide Order: The Argument Before the Artwork

The most important document in a pitch deck job is not the artwork. It is the story: the argument in order, from the problem to the ask, stated in the words the audience will understand. The designer lays the slides out on the story, keeping one point per slide, keeping text large enough to read at presentation distance, and keeping the evidence on the slide that makes the claim. Where the story is already agreed — the client supplies the order, the wording, and the figures — the design work starts from a known argument, and the cost is the cost of the slides on that argument. Where no story exists — for a new business, a new offer, or a pitch that has never been given — the story must be agreed before the slides are finished, and that step takes time, may need several drafts, and may add cost. A dry run, presenting the draft deck aloud to a friendly audience before the real meeting, is worth its cost for any deck that matters, because it is the only way to check, before the meeting, that the story holds in order, that the slides read at presentation distance, that the charts are understood at a glance, and that the presentation fits the time allowed. Story and structure must be chosen together with the audience. A deck for investors, a deck for buyers, and a deck for a team each need a different order, a different emphasis, and a different ending, and a story chosen for one audience will fail with another. Tell the designer who will be in the room, what decision they will make, and how much time the presentation has, before the structure is chosen, because the audience decides the structure, the structure decides the slide count, and the slide count decides part of the price. A deck that will be presented in ten minutes should be short, with few slides and one point each. A deck that will be sent to be read without a presenter should carry more explanation on each slide, because no speaker will be there to supply it, and it should be built as a reading deck from the start, not as a presentation deck with notes added afterwards. Each use points to a different structure, a different slide count, and a different price, and the choice should be made deliberately, once, before the artwork begins.

Content, Figures and Charts: The Work Before the Layout

A deck is only as good as the content and figures it is given to lay out, and the work of preparing them is often the largest hidden part of a deck job. A flyer needs a headline and a few details. A deck needs a story in order, a set of claims the presenter can defend, figures that have been checked, and charts that show those figures honestly. Each of those must be written for the slide the audience will meet it on, in the order the audience will meet it, not copied from a business plan in the order a plan presents it. One quote may assume the client will supply the story, the wording, and the figures, ready to lay out, while another may include time to shape the story, shorten the wording so that it fits the slides, and build the charts from raw figures. Both can be fair quotes, but they are quotes for different jobs, and the cheaper one is not cheaper if the story still needs to be written, the figures still need to be checked, or the charts still need to be built before the deck can be laid out. The same is true of visuals. A deck needs fewer images than a brochure, but each one must be sharp at presentation size, in the orientation the slide needs, and honest about the product or the result it shows. A photograph that looks sharp on a laptop can look soft or blocky when projected large if it was supplied too small, and a photograph supplied in the wrong orientation will either be cropped badly or leave the slide with a gap it cannot fill. Where a deck must show figures — market size, revenue, growth, costs, or any number the audience will test — the designer is not the person who decides what the figures are. The client must supply the exact figures and confirm that they are complete and correct, because a deck that states a figure wrongly cannot be fixed by good design, and may have to be corrected in the room, in front of the audience it was meant to persuade. Ask, before the work starts, who supplies the story, who checks the figures, who supplies the images, and who builds the charts. The answers will tell you whether the price covers the slide artwork alone, or the slide artwork plus the preparation that makes the deck persuasive. The cheapest deck is rarely the one with the cheapest artwork. It is the one whose story, figures and images were ready, in the right order, before the layout began, so the designer's time was spent designing rather than waiting, chasing, or rewriting.

Design Cost Versus Content, Coaching and Follow-Up: Separate Bills

The most common confusion in deck pricing is the belief that there is one price for a deck. In practice there are almost always several: the price of designing the slides, the price of developing the story and content, the price of any presentation coaching or rehearsal help, the price of follow-up material such as a one-page summary or a leave-behind document, and the price of any translation or adaptation for a second audience. The design fee pays for the slides: the layout, the order of the argument, the colours, the type, and the editable files. The content fee, where it applies, pays for shaping the story, shortening the wording, and checking that the argument holds in order. Coaching, where it is offered, pays for rehearsal, timing, and help with the questions the audience is likely to ask. Follow-up material is a separate deliverable: a summary that the audience can take away, or a version of the deck that can be read without a presenter, each built from the same story but laid out for a different use. Some designers bundle the story work with the design, and some designers include a first rehearsal review in their package, so check every quote to see whether story development, coaching, and follow-up material are included or whether you will pay for them separately. A quote that looks expensive because it includes the story work may be cheaper, in total, than a quote that looks cheap because it assumes the story is already finished. The same care applies to revisions. A quote should state how many revision rounds are included, what counts as a revision, and what happens when the story changes after the slides are built. A change of wording on a finished slide is a small revision. A change of story order, a new set of figures, or a new audience after the deck is built is a larger change, and it should be priced as one. Ask, before the work starts, what is included, what counts as a revision, and what will be charged if the brief changes. The answers will tell you the total cost of a finished, presentable deck, not only the cost of the first version of the slides.

How Slide Count, Revisions and Deadlines Change the Total

Slide count is the simplest driver of deck cost, and the easiest to misunderstand. More slides cost more, because each slide must be designed, checked, and fitted into the story, but the relationship is not a straight line. Ten slides that carry a clear story, with one point each, can be designed more quickly than twenty slides that carry the same story padded with detail, because the padded slides must be checked for repetition, for order, and for the audience's patience. The cheapest deck is often the shortest deck that makes the argument, not the longest deck the designer can build. A client who asks for more slides than the argument needs will pay for slides the audience will not remember, and will weaken the slides the audience would have remembered. Revisions change the total in a similar way. A deck built with two revision rounds, used well, will usually be better than a deck built with unlimited revisions, because unlimited revisions encourage the story to keep changing while the slides are being built, and a story that keeps changing cannot be laid out properly. Agree the revision rounds in advance, use the first round for the story and order, the second for the detail and the visuals, and treat a change of story after the second round as new work, priced as such. Deadlines change the total more sharply than either. A deck needed in a few days, built around other work, or built over a weekend, will cost more than the same deck built over two weeks, because the designer must set other work aside, work longer hours, and check the deck under pressure. A rush fee is not a penalty. It is the cost of speed, and a client who needs speed should expect to pay it, and should also expect that a rushed deck, however well designed, will have had less time for the dry run that catches the errors a careful deck avoids. Where the deadline is fixed — a meeting that cannot move — tell the designer at the start, agree what can be delivered in the time, and cut the scope rather than the checking: fewer slides, well checked, will serve the meeting better than more slides, unchecked, delivered on the morning of the meeting.

General Market Ranges for Pitch Deck Design

The ranges below are general market ranges for design alone, stated so that a buyer can judge a quote, not so that a buyer can budget from a range without a quote. A short custom deck of around ten slides, with the story and content supplied ready to lay out, typically runs from roughly two hundred dollars to around six hundred dollars. A standard investor or sales deck of around fifteen to twenty slides, with the story supplied but the wording shortened and the charts built by the designer, typically runs from roughly six hundred dollars to around two thousand dollars. A full fundraising deck, with story development, data visuals, several revision rounds, and a version that can be read without a presenter, typically costs more, because it covers the narrative, the research, and the follow-up material as well as the slides, and it is usually priced as a project rather than by the slide. A single-slide redesign, or a small refresh of an existing deck, typically costs less than a new deck, because the story and the structure already exist. A keynote deck for a large room, with large type and strong visuals, typically sits in the same range as a standard deck, but with more of the cost in the visuals and less in the text, because the slides must be understood from a distance. These ranges assume a designer working directly with the client, with the brand's colours and fonts available, and with the figures supplied and checked. They do not include story development from scratch, presentation coaching, printing, or translation, each of which is priced separately. They also do not include rush work, which adds to the price, or work by a large agency, which typically costs more than work by an independent designer for the same scope. A quote well below the range should be questioned as carefully as a quote well above it: a quote far below the range may assume the client will supply everything, may include only pictures of slides rather than editable files, or may include no revision rounds at all, and any of those will cost the buyer again when the deck needs to change. Always get a written quote for your exact deck, stating the slide count, the story and content work, the charts, the files, the revision rounds, and the deadline, before budgeting from any range.

What a Pitch Deck Design Package Should Include

A proper pitch deck package should include everything the buyer needs to present the deck, to update it, and to use it again. The finished slides should be supplied in an editable format, so that the buyer can change a figure, correct a name, or update a slide without paying for the whole deck again. The slides should follow a consistent system — layouts, colours, type, and chart styles that match the brand — so that a new slide added later looks like part of the same deck. The charts and diagrams should be built from the supplied figures, in a form the buyer can update, and should be checked for honesty: a chart that exaggerates a difference, or that hides a part of the picture, will be noticed by an audience that is testing the argument, and will damage the trust the rest of the deck has built. Speaker notes should be included where the deck will be presented live, written as the speaker will actually say them, with the timing and the transitions noted, so that the presenter is not left to improvise the connections between slides. A set of revision rounds, agreed in advance, should be included, with the first round for story and order and the later rounds for detail and visuals. A check of the deck as it will be presented should be included: the slides read at presentation size, the text is large enough to read from the back of the room, and the file opens and plays correctly on the device that will present it, including any video, animation, or linked file the deck depends on. A version for reading without a presenter should be included only where it was agreed, and should be built as a reading version, with the explanation on the slides, rather than as the presentation version with notes attached. A package that only includes pictures of slides, or a file that cannot be edited when the figures change, will cost the buyer again the first time the deck needs updating, and should not be accepted as a complete package. Check, before accepting a quote, exactly which files, which formats, and which rounds of revision are included, and keep the editable files, the fonts, and any images that the package includes, so that a future update or a small change does not mean paying for the whole design again.

How to Brief a Designer for a Pitch Deck

A good brief is the cheapest part of a deck job, and the part that saves the most money. It should answer, in a page or two, the questions the designer will need before the first slide is laid out. Who is the audience, and what decision will they make? What is the one argument the deck must make, stated in a sentence? What is the story, in order, from the problem to the ask? What figures must the deck show, and have they been checked? What images, brand colours, fonts, and existing material should the deck use? How many slides are expected, and how much time will the presentation have? What files are needed — an editable deck, a reading version, a summary — and by when? A brief that answers those questions will produce a quote that can be compared fairly, and a deck that is built once, properly, rather than rebuilt as the answers emerge. A brief that leaves those questions open will produce a quote that assumes the designer will answer them, and a deck that changes shape as the answers arrive, with each change priced as a revision. The brief should also say what the deck is not. It is not a business plan, it is not a brochure, and it is not a document to be read in detail. Saying what the deck is not will stop the designer from building the wrong kind of deck, and will stop the client from asking for a deck that tries to do every job at once. Supply the material with the brief, not after it: the story draft, the figures, the images, and the brand material, in the forms the designer can use. A figure supplied as a picture cannot be checked or updated; a figure supplied as a number can. An image supplied too small cannot be used at presentation size; an image supplied at the size it will be shown can. The brief should also name the person who will decide. A deck with several decision-makers, each of whom can change the story, will be revised more often than a deck with one decision-maker, and the quote should reflect that. Where the deck will be presented by someone other than the person who commissioned it, the presenter should be involved in the brief, or at least in the first dry run, because a deck built for one speaker will not suit another. A brief written well, supplied completely, and decided by one person will produce a deck that is built once, checked once, and presented with confidence. A brief written vaguely, supplied late, and decided by committee will produce a deck that is rebuilt several times, and paid for several times, before it is ever presented.

Mistakes That Cost Pitch Deck Buyers Money

The mistakes worth naming are the ones deck buyers repeat without noticing, because a deck looks simpler than it is. Asking for more slides than the argument needs, so that the audience remembers less, not more. Copying a business plan onto slides, so that the audience reads while the speaker talks, and hears neither properly. Using a template without rewriting the story, so that the deck follows the template's order instead of the argument's order, and looks like every other deck built from the same template. Putting the claim on a slide and the evidence on another, so that the audience must hold the claim in mind while waiting for the proof. Building charts from unchecked figures, so that a question from the audience exposes an error in the room. Accepting a quote without asking what is included — the editable files, the speaker notes, the revision rounds, the reading version — and discovering, after the deck is delivered, that the file cannot be edited when the figures change. Agreeing unlimited revisions, so that the story keeps changing while the slides are being built, and the deck is never finished. Leaving the dry run until the morning of the meeting, so that the errors a rehearsal would have caught are caught by the audience instead. And judging a quote only by its price, without asking whether it covers the story work, the charts, the checking, and the deadline, so that the cheapest quote becomes the most expensive deck once the missing work is added. Avoid those, brief the deck properly, check the figures, rehearse it aloud, and keep the editable files, and the deck will do the job it exists for: persuading the people in the room, in the order they need to be persuaded, and leaving them with an argument they remember and a next step they are ready to take.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does pitch deck design cost in 2026?
As a general market range, a short custom pitch deck of around ten slides typically runs from roughly two hundred dollars to around six hundred dollars, a standard investor or sales deck of around fifteen to twenty slides typically runs from roughly six hundred dollars to around two thousand dollars, and a full fundraising deck with story development, data visuals and several revision rounds typically costs more, because it covers the narrative and the research as well as the slides. The design fee is usually a one-off cost, separate from any presentation coaching, printing, or translation, and the total depends on the slide count, whether the story and wording are supplied or must be developed, the number of charts and diagrams, the revision rounds included, and the deadline. Always get a written quote for your exact deck before budgeting from a range.

Is pitch deck design charged per slide or per project?
Both ways are common, and the choice should be understood before accepting a quote. Per-slide pricing suits a deck whose slide count is fixed and whose content is supplied ready to lay out, because the price follows the number of slides. Per-project pricing suits a deck whose story still needs shaping, whose content is incomplete, or whose slide count may change as the story is developed, because the price covers the thinking as well as the slides. A per-slide quote that assumes finished content will not be a fair price for a deck that needs its story written, and a per-project quote should state what it includes — the story work, the number of slides, the charts, and the revision rounds — so that the buyer can compare it fairly with a per-slide quote. Ask which basis the quote uses, and what happens if the slide count changes.

What should a pitch deck design package include?
A proper package should include the finished slides in an editable format, a consistent slide system of layouts, colours and type that matches the brand, charts and diagrams built from the supplied figures, speaker notes where the deck will be presented live, and a set of revision rounds agreed in advance. It should also include a check of the deck as it will be presented: the slides read at presentation size, the text is large enough to read from the back of a room, and the file opens and plays correctly on the device that will present it. A package that only includes pictures of slides, or a file that cannot be edited when the figures change, will cost the buyer again the first time the deck needs updating. Check, before accepting a quote, exactly which files and which rounds of revision are included.

Can I use a template instead of hiring a pitch deck designer?
A template can be a reasonable starting point for a short internal deck, where the audience already knows the subject and the purpose is to share information rather than to persuade. A template will usually be the wrong choice for a fundraising or sales deck, because the template decides the story's shape before the story has been written: the slides follow the template's order, the charts follow the template's style, and the deck ends up looking like every other deck built from the same template, with the buyer's own argument fitted into someone else's structure. A designer builds the deck around the argument — the order of the slides, the evidence on each slide, and the visuals that carry the point — so that the deck persuades in the order the audience needs to be persuaded. Where a template is used, the buyer should still rewrite the story for their own audience, replace every chart with their own figures, and check that no template wording remains.
