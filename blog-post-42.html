BUSINESS CARD DESIGN PRICING

How Much Does Business Card Design Cost in 2026?

November 23, 2026 • 13 min read • By Muhammad Atif Iqbal

A business card looks like the smallest design job a business will ever buy, and that is exactly why its price is so often misunderstood. The card itself is only a few inches wide, the words on it can be counted on two hands, and the whole job can look, from the outside, like ten minutes of arranging a name and a phone number. In practice, a good card is a piece of print design that has to survive being held, pocketed, photographed, scanned and compared with a dozen other cards from the same meeting, and it has to do all of that at a size where a single millimetre of poor spacing is visible. The design fee pays for the judgement that makes that small rectangle work: what goes on the front and what, if anything, goes on the back, how large the name and the contact details should be, how much empty space the card needs to breathe, and how the finished artwork must be prepared so that the printer returns the card the designer intended, with nothing cut off and no colour surprise. This guide explains what business card design cost actually covers in 2026, the general market ranges for design alone, how card sizes, finishes and the choice between one side and two sides move the price, what a proper card design package includes, and how design and printing interact, so that the total cost of a finished, printed card is clear before anyone is paid. One thing to be clear about up front: the ranges in this post are general market ranges, not quotes and not a promise of any price. Your exact price depends on your card, your finishes and your printer, and any designer worth hiring will quote your job in writing after reading your brief.

The reason the question matters is that a business card is still, for many trades and professions, the first physical object a new contact takes away. A website can be updated in an afternoon and a social media profile can be edited at any time, but a printed card is fixed the moment the print run leaves the printer. If the phone number is wrong, if the email address is out of date, if the text is too small to read in a dim room, or if the card looks crowded and cheap beside the cards it will sit next to in a wallet, the business lives with that impression until the cards run out or are thrown away and reprinted. That permanence is what makes the design stage worth doing properly, even though the card is small. It is also what makes the cheapest route risky: a card assembled in a hurry from a generic template, with no check of the print settings, can look acceptable on a screen and disappointing in the hand, because a screen hides the very things print reveals — thin paper, washed-out colour, text that sits too close to the edge, and a finish that makes the card hard to write on. The useful question is therefore not "what is the cheapest card?" but "what should I pay for a card design that will be printed well, read easily, and still look right after a hundred handshakes?" The rest of this guide answers that question, starting with what business card design actually is, and what it is not.

What Business Card Design Actually Is

Business card design is the layout of a single, small printed rectangle so that a stranger can read it in seconds, remember the business, and reach the right person without effort. The designer decides the hierarchy: which piece of information is read first, which second, and which can wait until the reader turns the card over or scans the code on it. In most cases the person's name and the business name come first, because those are what the reader is trying to remember. The job title or service comes next, because it tells the reader why this card matters. The contact details — phone, email, website, address if the business has a premises worth visiting — come next, set large enough to read and spaced so that each one is distinct. Everything else is supporting material: a short line about the service, a code that links to a booking page or a portfolio, a social media handle, or a small piece of imagery that helps the card be recognised again. A designer who understands hierarchy will resist the temptation to fill every corner. Empty space on a card is not wasted space. It is what lets the eye find the name, and it is what keeps the card from looking like a flyer that has been shrunk until it is unreadable.

What business card design is not is a new brand identity. A card design normally works with artwork and colours that already exist: the business name, its existing mark, its colours and its fonts, supplied by the client or taken from an earlier brand package. If the business does not yet have those things, the card job becomes larger, because the designer must first settle the colours, the fonts and the arrangement that the card will then use — and that wider work is priced separately. That distinction matters when comparing quotes. One quote may assume the client will supply the artwork and the wording, ready to lay out, while another may include time to tidy the artwork, choose matching fonts, or redraw a mark that only exists as a small image file. Both can be fair quotes, but they are quotes for different jobs, and the cheaper one is not cheaper if the artwork still needs to be prepared before the card can be laid out. Ask, before you accept any quote, what the designer expects you to supply, and what the designer will prepare. The answer will tell you whether the price covers the card alone, or the card plus the preparation that makes the card possible. A clear answer at this stage avoids the most common card-design disappointment: a card that was designed around a low-resolution image, printed at a size where the image breaks up, because nobody checked the artwork before the layout began.

Card Sizes, Shapes and Orientation

The standard business card size in the United States is 3.5 by 2 inches, and the standard size in much of Europe is 85 by 55 millimetres, which is the size of a credit card. Those two sizes dominate because they fit wallets, card holders, and the trays and stands that businesses use at receptions and trade shows, and because printers stock paper and cutting tools for them. A card that matches the local standard is the safe, practical choice for most businesses: it fits where cards are expected to fit, it costs the standard print price, and it needs no special cutting. Before choosing a size, check which standard your printer and your audience actually use. A business that meets clients in both the United States and Europe, for example, may find that one size fits better in one market than the other, and it is worth deciding which market matters more, or whether two print runs in two sizes are justified. Do not assume that a size that looks right on a screen will feel right in the hand. Print one card at the intended size, hold it beside a card you already own, and check that the text is readable at arm's length, because that is the distance at which a card is usually first read.

Beyond the standard rectangle, printers commonly offer square cards, taller slim cards, folded cards that open like a small booklet, and cards with rounded corners or other shaped edges. Each of those choices changes both the design and the price. A square card gives the designer a different shape to balance, and it stands out in a stack of rectangles, but it also holds less room for a long website address or a long job title on a single line, so the wording must be planned for the shape. A slim, tall card suits a business that wants a modern look and has very little text, but it can feel cramped for a business that needs a phone number, two email addresses, an address and a website on the same card. A folded card gives four small panels instead of two sides, which suits a business that needs a short service list, a small map, or an appointment reminder on the card itself — but the design is now four layouts, not one, the paper must fold cleanly, and the printing and finishing cost rises accordingly. Rounded corners and shaped edges need a cutting step beyond the standard straight cut, and that step adds to the print cost on every card in the run. None of these options is wrong. They are all ways of making a card memorable, and a memorable shape can be worth paying for when the card must stand out at a busy event. The mistake is to choose the shape first and the wording second. Choose the wording first — what must the card say, and what can the back carry — and then choose the shape that holds that wording comfortably. A designer who is told the shape before the content will design a card that looks striking and reads badly. A designer who is told the content first will choose, or recommend, a shape that serves it.

Orientation is the quieter half of the same decision. Most cards are landscape, wider than they are tall, because that shape suits a name on one line, contact details below it, and a mark at one end. Portrait cards, taller than they are wide, suit a business whose name is long, whose mark is tall, or whose card is meant to be read while held upright, as a phone is held. The orientation should follow the content and the mark, not fashion. A landscape mark squeezed into a portrait card, or a portrait photograph cropped into a landscape card, will both look forced, and the designer will spend the layout trying to solve a problem that the orientation created. If you are unsure, ask the designer to show the same content in both orientations at the real size, on paper if possible, and choose the one you can read fastest. Speed of reading is the right test for a card, because a card is rarely studied. It is glanced at, pocketed, and read properly later, often in a different place and a different light from the one in which it was handed over.

Single-Sided or Double-Sided: What the Back Is For

A single-sided card carries everything on one face and leaves the back blank, or printed in a plain colour. A double-sided card uses both faces. The choice is not a matter of getting more for the money. It is a matter of what the card must do, and how much the reader should be asked to do. A single-sided card is the clearest card there is: everything the reader needs is in front of them, there is nothing to turn over, and the blank back is a useful surface — a place to write a note, a price, a date, or a reminder of the conversation. That matters more than it sounds. At a market stall, a clinic, or a trade stand, the person who receives the card often writes on the back immediately: a measurement, a date, a name to ask for. A card with a full design on the back cannot be written on easily, especially if the back has a dark background or a glossy finish. If your card is likely to be written on, a single-sided card, or a double-sided card with a deliberately plain, writable area on the back, is the practical choice, and the designer should be told so before the layout begins, because a writable back needs an uncoated surface, which is a paper and finish decision, not just a design decision.

A double-sided card, by contrast, is the right choice when the front alone cannot carry what the card must say without becoming crowded. The usual arrangement is a front that carries the identity — the name, the business, the mark, and the single most important contact route — and a back that carries the rest: the full contact details, a short list of services, a photograph of the work, a map or directions, a code that links to a booking page, or a line that explains what the business does for people who did not hear the conversation. The back can also carry a design that makes the card memorable: a large pattern, a photograph, a colour block, or a single strong line. What the back should not do is repeat the front. A back that repeats the name, the phone number and the website in the same arrangement as the front adds nothing, and it wastes the one extra surface the card has. Designing a double-sided card costs more than designing a single-sided one, because the back is a second layout that must be balanced, aligned with the front, and proofed separately, even when the back is simpler than the front. Printing it also usually costs more, because both sides must be printed and registered so that the two sides line up when the card is cut. The extra cost is often modest for a standard finish, and it is usually justified when the back carries real content. It is not justified when the back carries a repeat of the front. Before you ask for a quote, write down what the back must do in one sentence. If you cannot write that sentence, the card probably only needs one side, and the money saved on the second layout can go to better paper or a better finish on the one side that matters.

Paper, Thickness, Finishes and Special Effects

The paper a card is printed on is part of the design, not an afterthought, because the reader feels the card before reading it. Card stock is usually described by its weight or thickness, and a heavier stock feels more substantial in the hand, holds its shape in a wallet, and takes a finish more cleanly than a thin stock. A very thin card can feel flimsy beside the cards it will be compared with, and it can curl or crease in a pocket. A very thick card can feel impressive, but it can also be hard to write on, hard to fit in a tight card holder, and more expensive to post if the card is ever mailed. The sensible approach is to handle samples before committing to a print run. Ask the printer for samples of the stocks and finishes you are considering, at the card size you intend to use, and compare them in your hand, not only on a screen. A finish that looks subtle in a photograph can feel striking in the hand, and a finish that looks striking in a photograph can feel slippery or hard to read in real light. The designer should know the stock and finish before the final artwork is made, because some finishes change how colour and fine detail appear, and the artwork must be prepared for the finish that will actually be used.

The common finishes each do a different job. An uncoated, matte stock has no shine, feels like good writing paper, takes a pen easily, and suits a business that wants a quiet, natural, or craft feel — a clinic, a consultancy, a maker, a bookshop. A gloss or silk-coated stock has a sheen, makes photographs look richer, and resists marks and moisture better than uncoated stock, but it is harder to write on and can glare under bright light. A soft-touch finish, sometimes called velvet lamination, feels smooth and slightly rubbery, and it gives a card a luxury feel that is noticed immediately on handling, but it adds a lamination step and a cost to every card. Beyond the basic finishes, printers offer special effects: foil stamping, where a metallic foil is pressed onto part of the design; embossing or debossing, where part of the design is raised or pressed into the card; spot gloss, where a glossy varnish is applied to one part of an otherwise matte card, so that a mark or a pattern catches the light; and edge painting, where the cut edges of the card are coloured. Each of those effects needs its own artwork layer — a separate file or mask that tells the printer exactly where the foil, the emboss or the varnish should go — and each adds a production step, a check, and a cost. A single special effect, used sparingly on one element, can make a card memorable. Several effects on the same card can fight each other, make the card hard to read, and add cost without adding clarity. Choose one effect, if any, that serves the card's purpose, and let the rest of the card be calm. A card with a foil mark and a quiet layout will be remembered. A card with foil, embossing, spot gloss and painted edges will be remembered for the wrong reason: it will look as if it is trying too hard.

Finishes also affect the design file, and this is where a card can go wrong even when the design looks perfect on a screen. Foil and embossing need clean, simple shapes with enough space around them, because a fine line or a small letter in foil can fill in or break up when it is pressed. Spot gloss needs a mask that matches the artwork exactly, and a mask that is a fraction out of alignment will show as a glossy edge where no gloss was intended. Dark, full-bleed backgrounds — colour that runs to the very edge of the card — show every tiny shift in cutting, because a white hairline at the edge is visible on a dark card and invisible on a white one. None of this means dark cards or special finishes should be avoided. It means the designer and the printer should agree, before the artwork is finished, on the stock, the finish, the bleed, and the safe margin for text, and the artwork should then be built to those agreed settings. A quote that includes the finish and the print preparation is a fuller quote than one that prices the layout alone and leaves the finish to be sorted out later. Ask whether the design fee includes preparing the special-effect layers and checking the printer's proof. If it does not, those steps will need to be paid for separately, or done by the printer, and the card should not be ordered until they are done.

Design Cost Versus Printing Cost: Two Separate Bills

The most common confusion in business card pricing is the belief that there is one price for a business card. In practice there are almost always two: the price of designing the card, and the price of printing it. The design fee is usually a one-off payment. It pays for the designer's time: understanding the brief, arranging the content, choosing and setting the type, placing the artwork, preparing the print-ready files, and making the agreed revision rounds. Once the design is approved and the files are delivered, the design fee is done, and a reprint of the same card should not need a new design fee, unless the content changes. The printing fee is a per-run payment. It pays for the paper, the ink, the finish, the cutting, and the labour of producing the number of cards ordered, and it is paid again every time a new batch is printed. Some printers bundle a basic layout service with the print order, usually from a template library, and some designers include a first print run in their package price. Both arrangements are legitimate, but they must be read carefully. A bundled layout is not the same as a custom design, and a package that includes printing is only good value if the paper, finish and quantity it includes are the ones the business actually wants. Always ask, in plain words: does this price include the design, the printing, or both, and if it includes printing, how many cards, on what stock, with what finish?

Separating the two bills also makes the long-term cost clearer. A custom design that costs more up front can be the cheaper choice over two or three years, because the design is paid for once and the reprints cost only the printing. A cheap template layout that is tied to one printer, in one file format that cannot be taken elsewhere, can become expensive when the business changes printer, changes a phone number, or wants the same card in a different size, because the layout must then be remade. The design files matter here, and they are covered in detail below. For now, the practical rule is simple: when comparing quotes, compare design with design and printing with printing. A quote that looks expensive because it includes five hundred printed cards is not comparable with a quote that prices the design alone. A quote that looks cheap because it prices the design alone is not comparable with a quote that includes the printing. Put the two parts side by side, in the same table, with the same quantity, the same stock and the same finish, and the comparison will be honest. If a quote does not separate the two parts, ask for the separation. A designer or printer who will not separate them is asking you to buy without knowing what you are buying.

How Quantity and Printing Change the Total

Printing quantity changes the total in a way that surprises many first-time buyers, because the relationship is not a straight line. The design fee does not change with quantity: designing a card that will be printed one hundred times costs the same as designing a card that will be printed one thousand times, because the layout is the same. The printing fee, however, falls sharply per card as the quantity rises, because the fixed costs of setting up the print job — preparing the press or the digital file, setting the paper, checking the first sheets, setting the cutter — are spread across more cards. The first hundred cards therefore carry a high cost per card, the next few hundred carry a much lower one, and by the time the run reaches a thousand cards, the cost per card is often a small fraction of what it was for the first hundred. That is why printers quote in bands — commonly one hundred, two hundred and fifty, five hundred, and one thousand cards — and why the jump from one band to the next can look like very good value. It is good value only if the cards will be used. A thousand cards that sit in a drawer because the phone number changed, the address changed, or the business changed its name are not cheap cards. They are wasted cards, and the money spent printing them would have been better spent on a smaller run and a second, updated run later.

The sensible quantity is therefore a judgement, not a calculation. A new business that is still testing its wording, its phone number, or even its name should print a small run — enough to use for a few months, not enough to regret. A business with settled details, a steady flow of meetings, and a card that is handed out daily can justify a larger run, because the per-card saving is real and the cards will be used before anything changes. A business with several staff members should think in the same way, person by person: a card for a person whose details are settled is worth a larger run, while a card for a new starter, or a role that may change, is worth a smaller one. Reprints also deserve a line in the budget. A card design that is kept as a proper print-ready file, with the editable source file held by the business or the designer, can be reprinted by any competent printer, with only the printing to pay for. A card that exists only as a low-resolution image, or only in a printer's online template system, cannot be reprinted elsewhere without being remade, and the remake will cost design time again. When you order the first run, ask how a reprint will work, what file will be used, and whether a small change — a new phone number, a new job title — will be charged as a small edit or as a new design. The answer is part of the true cost of the card, even though it will not be paid until later.

Special finishes and non-standard sizes change the quantity calculation too. A standard card on a standard stock, with a standard finish, is the cheapest to print at any quantity, because the printer's setup is routine. A card with foil, embossing, a special shape, or a very thick stock needs extra setup, extra checks, and sometimes a minimum order, and the extra cost is spread across the run in the same way as the basic setup cost — so a small run of a special card can be very expensive per card, while a larger run of the same card can be reasonable. If the budget only stretches to a small run, a standard size and a standard finish, with the money spent on the design and the paper quality, will usually give a better card than a small run of a special card that has been cut down to fit the budget. If the special finish is the point of the card — if the card is meant to be kept, shown and talked about — then the larger run that makes the finish affordable is worth considering, provided the details on the card are settled and will not change. Either way, get the printing quote in writing, for the exact quantity, stock, finish and size, before approving the design. A design approved for a finish the budget cannot afford will have to be redesigned, and that redesign is the most avoidable cost in the whole card project.

General Market Ranges by Tier

The ranges below are what you will typically see across online print services, freelance marketplaces, designer portfolios and studio pricing pages. They are general market ranges for the design work, not quotes, and they do not include printing unless the text says so. The exact price for your card depends on whether it is single-sided or double-sided, whether the artwork is supplied ready to use, how many versions and revision rounds are included, and whether special finishes need extra artwork. Treat these as a map, not a price list, and always get a written quote for your exact card.

Template or online print-service layout: roughly $0 to around $50, often bundled with printing. You choose a template, type your details into it, and the printer prints the result. The design work is selection and typing, not custom layout, and the result can be perfectly serviceable for a straightforward card with standard details. The trade-offs are the ones that apply to any template: the layout is shared with other businesses, the fonts and spacing cannot be adjusted far, and the file usually stays inside the printer's system, so a reprint elsewhere means starting again. This tier suits a card that is needed urgently, a card for a short-lived purpose, or a business that wants to test its wording before paying for a custom design. It does not suit a card that must look distinct, carry a photograph or a special finish, or match an existing brand precisely.

Custom design by a freelancer: roughly $50 to $300. A designer lays out the card for this business alone, working from the supplied artwork and wording, with one or two design versions and one or two revision rounds, and delivers print-ready files. This is the tier where most small-business cards land, and it is usually the best value for a standard card: the layout is original, the type is set for the card's real size, the files are prepared for the printer, and the card can be reprinted from those files. The range is wide because a single-sided card with supplied artwork and one revision round is a smaller job than a double-sided card with a photograph, a code, special finishes and several revision rounds. A card that needs the artwork tidied, the wording edited, or several versions for several staff members will sit at the higher end of this tier, or just above it, and that is fair, because each of those is real work. Check the portfolio for printed cards, not only screen mockups, and ask whether the price includes the print-ready files and the revision rounds you expect.

Card design within a small studio package: roughly $300 to $1,000, usually as part of wider work. A studio rarely prices a single card in isolation at this tier. The card is designed as part of a brand package — the stationery set, the social media templates, the email signature, the letterhead — with a set process, more than one person checking the work, and a brand guide that keeps the card consistent with everything else. The card itself may be one item in a package that costs several hundred dollars or more, and judging it by the card alone will make it look expensive. Judged as part of the package, it is the cost of consistency: the same colours, fonts and spacing on the card, the letterhead and the website, checked by a team. This tier suits a business that is launching or rebranding and needs the card to match a new identity from the first day, rather than a business that already has a settled identity and only needs one card laid out.

Agency or full identity system: roughly $1,000 and above, as part of a full brand project. At this level the card is one application of a full identity system, designed by a team over weeks, with research, strategy, and guidelines that govern every place the identity appears. The card's share of that project is not a meaningful number on its own, because the project is buying the system, not the card. A small business rarely needs this tier for a card alone, and it should not buy a full identity project only to get a card. It is included here so that the range is honest: the top of the market is not buying a card, it is buying a brand, and the card is one small, carefully governed part of it. If a business is quoted a four-figure price for a single card with no wider identity work, ask what the price covers. It may be justified by special finishes, several versions, or a very complex card, but it should be explained, not simply stated.

Printing, for comparison, is a separate general market range, and it is worth stating here so that the design ranges above are not mistaken for the total. As a general market range, a standard run of a few hundred plain cards on a standard stock, with a standard finish, often costs from a few tens of dollars to around one or two hundred dollars, depending on the quantity, the stock and the printer. A run with a special finish, a thick stock, a shaped edge or a folded format costs more, and a very small run costs more per card than a larger run of the same card. Those printing figures are broad, they vary by country and by printer, and they are not a quote. The point of stating them is to show the shape of the total: for a standard card, the design and the printing are often of a similar order of magnitude, and neither should be budgeted without the other. A business that budgets only for the design will be surprised by the printing bill, and a business that budgets only for the printing will be surprised by the design bill. Budget for both, in writing, before the work starts.

What a Card Design Package Should Include

A proper card design package should be judged by its file list, not by its mockup. The mockup — the card shown on a screen, on a desk, or in a hand — is how the design is presented, and it is useful for judging the look. It is not the deliverable. The deliverable is the set of files that lets the card be printed correctly, shared correctly, and changed correctly in the future. At a minimum, the package should include a print-ready PDF, made to the printer's specification, with the card at its true size, with bleed, with trim marks if the printer asks for them, and in the colour mode the printer requires. Bleed is the extra printed area beyond the card's cut edge, usually a few millimetres on each side, which ensures that colour running to the edge is not left with a white hairline if the cutting shifts by a fraction. A file without bleed, for a card with colour to the edge, is not print-ready, whatever it is called. The package should also include a screen version of the card — usually a JPG or PNG — for email, messaging, and online use, so that the card can be shared without sending the print file. And it should state, in writing, how many design versions were offered, how many revision rounds are included, and what happens if a further round is needed.

Beyond the minimum, there are three items worth asking for, and worth paying for if they are not included. The first is the editable source file, or a clear statement of whether it is included and what it may be used for. The source file is the working file the designer built the card in, and it is what another designer needs to change a phone number, add a staff member, or adapt the card to a new size without rebuilding it. Some designers include it, some charge extra for it, and some retain it and offer small edits as a service. All three arrangements are legitimate, but the arrangement should be known before the work starts, because a card without its source file is a card that must be remade for every change. The second is the special-effect artwork, if the card uses foil, embossing or spot gloss: the separate masks or layers the printer needs, checked against the main artwork, so that the effect lands exactly where it was designed to land. The third is a short written note of the card's settings: the true size, the bleed, the colour mode, the fonts used, and the printer's specification the file was made to. That note costs the designer a few minutes and saves the next person — a different printer, a different designer, or the business itself — from guessing. A package that includes the print-ready file, the screen version, the source-file arrangement in writing, and the settings note is a complete package. A package that includes only a screen image, however handsome, is not a package at all. It is a picture of a card.

The print-ready file also needs to be right in the technical details that a screen mockup hides. The colour mode matters: print files are usually prepared in CMYK, the four-ink process printers use, while screen files use RGB, the light-based mode screens use, and a file prepared in the wrong mode can print with colours that look duller or shifted from the screen version. The resolution matters: photographs and any raster artwork on the card should be at a high resolution at the card's true size — commonly 300 dots per inch is quoted as the print standard — because a photograph that looks sharp on a phone screen can look soft or blocky when printed at card size if it was supplied too small. The fonts matter: text should be embedded in the PDF or converted to outlines, so that the printer's computer does not substitute a different font when the file is opened. And the safe margin matters: text and important details should sit a few millimetres inside the cut edge, so that a tiny shift in cutting does not clip a letter or a phone number. A designer who checks all four — colour mode, resolution, fonts and safe margin — before delivering the file is doing the part of the job that the client cannot see and the printer will notice immediately. Ask the designer what checks they run before delivery, and ask the printer to confirm the file when it arrives. A printer who says the file is wrong is not being difficult. The printer is the last person who can catch a problem before it is printed five hundred times.

How to Brief a Designer for a Business Card

The fastest way to get an accurate quote — and a good card — is a brief that answers the questions the designer will otherwise have to guess. A good card brief is short, but it is complete. It states the person's name exactly as it should appear, including any spelling, capitalisation or professional title that must be correct. It states the business name exactly as it should appear, and whether the business name and the person's name are the same or different. It lists every contact detail to be included, in full, with the phone number in the format it should be dialled, the email address checked letter by letter, and the website address checked to confirm that it loads and that it is the address the business wants people to use. It states the job title or service line, in the words the business uses with customers, not in internal jargon. It states what should go on the back, if anything, in one sentence, and whether the back should be left writable. It states the card size and orientation, or asks the designer to recommend one, and it states the stock and finish, or asks the printer to quote the options. And it states the quantity to be printed, or the quantity range, so that the designer and the printer can both quote the real job. A brief that contains those answers will produce a quote that can be compared with another quote for the same job. A brief that omits them will produce quotes that cannot be compared, because each designer will have assumed a different card.

The brief should also say what to supply, and in what form. Supply the existing artwork as the best file you have — the original vector or high-resolution file if you have it, not a small image copied from a website or a social media profile, because a small image will not print cleanly at card size, however good it looks on a screen. Supply the colours and fonts if the business has a brand guide, or say that there is no guide and the designer should work from the existing materials. Supply the exact wording, proofread, rather than asking the designer to draft it from a conversation, because a phone number or an email address taken down wrong in a conversation will be printed wrong on every card. And supply any code, link or handle that must appear on the card, tested, so that the designer places a code that works rather than a code that was typed from memory. If the card must match a website, it is worth linking the designer to that website in the brief. Our guide to website redesign costs in 2026 is useful context when the card and the website are being refreshed together, so that the two are planned as one presence rather than two separate jobs that happen to share a name. The same brief, sent to every designer you contact, is what makes the quotes comparable. Send a different brief to each designer, and the quotes will be for different cards, however similar they look.

What to put on the card, and what to leave off, is the hardest part of the brief, because the temptation is to include everything. A card that lists every service, every social media account, every branch address and a paragraph about the business is not informative. It is unreadable. The test for every item is simple: will the person who receives this card need this item to reach the business, to remember what it does, or to decide to make contact? A phone number, an email address and a website pass that test for most businesses. A second phone number passes it only if it is genuinely used. A social media handle passes it if the business actually answers messages there. A QR code passes it if it links to something the reader wants in the moment — a booking page, a menu, a portfolio, a map — and if the code is large enough and clear enough to scan from a printed card held at arm's length. A long list of services does not pass the test on the front of the card, though a short list of three or four services can work on the back, where there is room for it. An address passes it if customers visit the premises, and fails it if they never do. A slogan passes it if it helps the reader remember what the business does, and fails it if it merely repeats the business name. Be ruthless with the front. Put the name, the business, the service in a few words, and the best way to reach the person. Put the rest, if it earns its place, on the back. And leave enough empty space for the card to be read at a glance. A card that is read at a glance will be kept. A card that must be studied will be put down.

Checking the Proof Before Anything Is Printed

The proof is the last chance to catch an error before it is printed hundreds of times, and it deserves the same care as the design itself. When the designer sends the proof, check it in the order a stranger would read it, not in the order you wrote the brief. Read the name first, letter by letter, and compare it with an official document, not with your memory of how it is spelled. Read the phone number digit by digit, and dial it, or have someone else dial it, to confirm that it reaches the right person. Read the email address letter by letter, and send a test message to it. Open the website address from the proof, by typing it exactly as it appears, and confirm that it loads the right page. Scan any code on the proof with a phone, from a printed copy if possible, and confirm that it opens the right page. Check the job title, the business name, and any address in the same way, against a source that is not the brief itself, because an error copied from the brief into the design will survive every check that only compares the proof with the brief. Two people checking the proof is better than one, because the person who wrote the wording will read what they meant to write, while a fresh reader will read what is actually there. Give the proof a night if you can. An error that is invisible at midnight is often obvious in the morning, and a print run ordered in haste is a print run that cannot be recalled.

Beyond the wording, check the proof as a printed object. Ask the designer or the printer for a proof at the true size, on paper if possible, and hold it at arm's length in ordinary light. Is the smallest text readable without squinting? Is the name the largest thing on the card, or is it fighting with a mark, a pattern or a photograph for attention? Is there enough space inside the cut edge that a tiny shift in cutting would not clip a letter? Does the back align with the front in the way it should — does the card feel balanced when it is turned over, or does the back seem to belong to a different card? Does the colour on the proof match the colour you expected, allowing for the fact that a screen and a printed sheet never match exactly? If the card uses a special finish, has the printer confirmed that the effect artwork matches the main artwork, and has a sample or a proof of the effect been seen? None of these checks takes long, and together they take far less time than reprinting a run. Approve the proof in writing, with the date, so that there is a clear record of what was approved. A written approval protects both sides: the printer knows the file that was approved, the designer knows the design that was approved, and the business knows the card that was ordered. If the printed cards differ from the approved proof, the written approval is the document that settles the question. If there was no written approval, the question is settled by memory, and memory is a poor record for a print job.

Red Flags and Smart Ways to Save

A few warning signs are worth knowing before you pay anyone. Be cautious of a designer who will not show printed cards they have designed, or whose examples are all screen mockups, because a card that has never been printed has never been tested where it matters. Be cautious of a quote that does not say whether the price includes print-ready files, how many revision rounds are included, or whether the source file is included, because those three omissions are where cheap quotes usually hide their limits. Be cautious of a price that includes no proofing step, or a printer who will print without a proof, because the proof is the only protection against a printed error. Be cautious of artwork supplied only as a small image file, with no check of its resolution, because the card will be printed from that file and the result will show. Be cautious of a card design that must be re-bought for every staff member, with no arrangement for additional names on the same layout, because a business with several staff will pay the full design fee several times for what should be a small edit. And be cautious of anyone who promises that a card alone will bring customers. A card can help a business be remembered and reached, but it cannot, on its own, bring the customers. None of these mean the designer or printer is dishonest. They usually mean the package is smaller than it looks, and you should check before you buy.

As for saving money without cutting corners, the best saving is a complete brief, because it avoids revision rounds spent discovering what the card should have said. The second is to settle the details before ordering: a small first run, with the wording tested in use, costs less in the long run than a large run of a card whose phone number or website changes a month later. The third is to buy the design once and reprint from the same files, rather than paying for a new layout for every reprint or every staff member — ask, before the first card is designed, what an additional name or a small change will cost. The fourth is to choose the finish honestly: a standard stock and finish, well designed, will serve most businesses better than a special finish on a card whose layout is crowded or whose text is too small. The fifth is to bundle the card with the other stationery the business needs, if it needs it — the letterhead, the email signature, the social media templates — because a designer who already knows the brand can produce those faster and more consistently than a new designer starting from scratch, and the set will match. And the sixth is timing: a card ordered without a deadline, with the proof checked properly, will cost less in reprints than a card ordered in a rush, approved without checking, and printed twice. A business card is a small purchase. Treated as a small purchase, it will be bought twice. Treated as the permanent, physical introduction it actually is, it will be bought once, and bought well.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does business card design cost in 2026?
As a general market range, a template-based card design typically costs very little or nothing beyond the printing, a custom card design from a freelancer typically runs from roughly fifty dollars to a few hundred dollars, and a card designed as part of a studio branding package typically costs more because the price covers the wider identity work as well. The design fee is usually a one-off cost, separate from printing, and the total depends on whether the card is single-sided or double-sided, the finishes chosen, the number of design versions, and the revision rounds included. Always get a written quote for your exact card before budgeting from a range.

Is business card design charged separately from printing?
Usually, yes. Design and printing are two different jobs, and they are usually charged separately. The design fee pays for the layout, the typography, the arrangement of your details, and the print-ready files. The printing fee pays for the paper or card stock, the finish, the cutting, and the number of cards produced. Some printers bundle a basic template layout with the print order, and some designers include a first print run in their package, so check every quote to see whether printing is included or whether you will pay the printer separately.

Does double-sided printing cost more than single-sided?
Designing a double-sided card usually costs more than designing a single-sided card, because the back is a second layout to design, balance and proof, even when it is simpler than the front. Printing a double-sided card also usually costs more than printing the same card on one side only, because both sides must be printed and aligned. The difference is often modest for standard finishes, but it grows when the back carries a full design, a photograph, or a special finish. Decide what the back must do before you ask for a quote, so the designer can price the real job.

What files should I receive for a business card?
You should receive a print-ready PDF with bleed, trim marks and the correct colour mode for the printer you will use, plus a screen version for email or messaging. Ask for the exact card size, the bleed allowance, the resolution and the colour mode to be confirmed in writing before the files are made, because a file made for one printer or one card size may not suit another. Keep the print-ready file, the screen version, and any editable source file that the package includes, so a future reprint or a small change does not mean paying for the whole design again.
