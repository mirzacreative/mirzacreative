Website Cost in 2026: What Small Businesses Actually Pay

November 14, 2026 • 6 min read • By Muhammad Atif Iqbal

Ask ten people what a website costs and you will get ten different answers — from "a few hundred dollars" to "tens of thousands". Both can be true, because a website is not one product. A simple five-page site and an online shop with a hundred products are different jobs with different prices, and the cheapest quote is rarely the cheapest website once you count the fixes, the missing pages, and the rebuild a year later. This guide breaks down what small businesses actually pay in 2026, what drives the price, and how to compare quotes fairly.

One note before the numbers: every price below is a realistic range based on what freelancers and small agencies typically charge, not a fixed quote. Your price depends on your pages, your content, your market, and who builds it. Treat the ranges as a way to spot a fair quote and a suspicious one — not as a price list.

What Actually Drives the Price

Six things decide most of the cost of a website, and understanding them is the fastest way to read a quote properly.

Design. A site built on a ready-made template with your colours and logo costs far less than a custom design built from scratch. Templates are a sensible choice for most small businesses; custom design is worth it when your brand needs to stand out in a crowded market or your layout is genuinely unusual.

Number of pages. Each page needs design, content, images and testing. A five-page site (home, about, services, portfolio, contact) is a different job from a twenty-page site with a blog, a gallery and a booking system. More pages means more hours, and the price should reflect that.

Copy and content. Writing is the part most quotes underestimate. If the provider writes your copy, that is real work and real cost; if you write it yourself, the price drops but the project waits on you. A site with no clear copy does not convert, however good it looks.

SEO setup. Basic SEO setup — page titles, descriptions, headings, sitemaps, speed, mobile layout — should be part of any professional build. A site built without it will need the work done later, usually at a higher price, because fixing a finished site is slower than building it right the first time.

Features and integrations. A contact form is cheap. A booking system, a payment gateway, a customer login area, or a multilingual version each add cost, testing and maintenance. List the features you actually need before you ask for a quote, and leave the rest for later.

Hosting and maintenance. A website is not a one-off purchase. Hosting, domain renewal, security updates and small fixes cost something every year. A quote that ignores the running costs is not a complete quote — ask what the first year of hosting and maintenance costs before you sign anything.

Realistic Price Ranges in 2026

A simple 5-page business website. For a small business site with a home page, about, services, portfolio or gallery, and contact page, built on a template with light customisation, a freelancer typically charges in the low hundreds to around one to two thousand dollars, depending on the market and the quality of the copy and design. An agency for the same site usually starts higher, often in the low thousands, because you are paying for a team, a process, and a project manager. A DIY builder can cost far less in cash — often a monthly subscription plus your own time — but the hours you spend are still a cost, and the result depends on your own design sense.

An online shop. A shop costs more, because every product is a page, and payments, shipping, taxes and returns all need to work flawlessly. A small shop with a dozen products, built by a freelancer on a platform like Shopify or WooCommerce, typically costs several times a simple brochure site. A larger shop with dozens of products, custom checkout rules, or integrations with accounting and stock systems can cost many thousands. The product count and the payment setup are the two biggest cost drivers — ask for a quote that prices them separately, so you can see what you are paying for.

Ongoing costs. Budget for hosting, domain renewal, security and small updates every year — typically a modest annual amount for a simple site, and more for a shop with regular product changes. Some providers offer a monthly care plan that covers updates, backups and small edits; for a business that cannot afford downtime, that plan is usually worth it.

DIY vs Freelancer vs Agency

DIY with a website builder is the cheapest in cash and the most expensive in your time. It works well if you have design sense, a simple site, and time to spare. It works badly if you need the site to sell, rank, or look professional in a competitive market — most DIY sites look like DIY sites, and customers notice.

A freelancer is the middle ground most small businesses choose: one person who designs, builds and often writes, at a price between DIY and agency. You get a personal service and a single point of contact, and a good freelancer will tell you honestly when a feature is unnecessary. The risk is capacity — one person can be slow during busy periods — so agree on a timeline in writing.

An agency costs the most and gives you a team: a designer, a developer, a copywriter and a project manager. It makes sense for larger sites, shops, or businesses that need the site to be a serious sales channel from day one. For a simple five-page site, an agency is usually more than you need.

Red Flags in Cheap Quotes

A very low quote is not a bargain if it leaves out the things you need. Watch for these signs.

  • No copy included. A site with placeholder text is not a finished site, and writing the copy yourself after launch usually means the site sits half-finished for months.
  • No SEO setup at all. If the quote does not mention titles, descriptions, speed or mobile layout, you will pay for that work later.
  • No revision rounds. Two or three revision rounds is a reasonable standard. A quote with no revisions means every change is charged extra.
  • You do not own the site. If the provider keeps the domain, the hosting account, or the design files, you cannot move the site elsewhere. Get ownership in writing.
  • No maintenance or update plan. Websites need updates. A provider who disappears after launch leaves you with a site that slowly breaks.
  • A price that is a fraction of every other quote. If five quotes cluster around a similar range and one is a tenth of the price, that quote is missing something — usually quality, revisions, or support.

Questions to Ask Before You Pay

Before you accept any quote, ask five questions. What exactly is included — pages, copy, images, SEO setup, revisions? Who owns the domain, the hosting and the design files? What happens when you need a change after launch, and what does it cost? How long will the build take, and what do you need to provide by when? And can you see two or three live sites the provider built for businesses like yours? The answers tell you more about the provider than the price does. A provider who answers clearly is usually a provider who delivers clearly.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a small business website cost in 2026?
It depends on the size and who builds it. A simple five-page site from a freelancer typically costs in the low hundreds to around one to two thousand dollars, while an agency usually starts higher. An online shop costs several times more because of products, payments and shipping. Always get a quote that lists what is included.

Is a cheap website worth it?
Sometimes, if the scope is genuinely small and everything you need is included. But a very cheap quote often leaves out copy, SEO setup, revisions or ownership, and fixing those gaps later usually costs more than paying fairly in the first place.

Should I build my website myself or hire someone?
Build it yourself if you have time, design sense and a simple site. Hire a freelancer if you want a professional result without agency prices, and an agency if the site is a major sales channel. The right choice depends on your budget, your timeline and how much the website matters to your revenue.

What are the ongoing costs of a website?
Hosting, domain renewal, security updates and small fixes every year. For a simple site that is a modest annual amount; for a shop it is more, especially if products change often. Ask for the first-year running cost in writing before you sign.
