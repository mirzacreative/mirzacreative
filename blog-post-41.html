How Much Does a Website Redesign Cost in 2026?

November 21, 2026 • 10 min read • By Muhammad Atif Iqbal

Every business owner with an old website asks the same question sooner or later, usually after a customer says the site looks dated, or after a competitor launches something better: how much will it cost to redesign it? The answers they find are rarely helpful, because they range from a few hundred dollars to tens of thousands, with no explanation of why. The range is real, and it is not a trick. A redesign can mean a fresh coat of paint on five pages, or a complete rebuild of a large site with new design, new content, new features and a careful move of every page that search engines already know. Those are different jobs, and they cost different amounts. This guide explains what a redesign actually includes, the six things that move the price, the general market ranges at each tier, the extra costs that quotes often leave out, and how to get an accurate quote for your own site. One thing to be clear about up front: the ranges in this post are general market ranges, not quotes and not a promise of any price. Your exact price depends on your site and your scope, and any designer worth hiring will quote your job in writing after looking at the site you already have.

The reason the question matters is that a website is rarely just a website. It is the first impression for most new customers, the place enquiries come from, and often the page a social media profile or a business listing points to. A site that looks old, loads slowly, or reads badly on a phone will lose visitors before they ever read what the business offers — and a redesign is how that first impression is fixed. But a redesign is also the easiest website job to buy badly, because the cheapest quote is often the one that leaves out the work that matters: the content, the search migration, the mobile testing, or the follow-up after launch. A business that buys on price alone can end up with a pretty site that ranks worse than the old one, or a site that looks new and still does not bring enquiries. The useful question is therefore not "what is the cheapest redesign?" but "what should I pay for the redesign my site actually needs?" The rest of this guide answers that question, starting with what the words refresh, redesign and rebuild actually mean — because the first saving is buying the right one of the three.

Refresh, Redesign or Rebuild: Which One Do You Need?

A refresh keeps the site's platform, structure and most of its content, and changes the look: new colours, new fonts, new images, a tidier homepage, and fixes to the pages that look worst on a phone. It is the right choice when the site's content is good, the platform suits the business, and only the appearance is letting it down. A refresh is the fastest and cheapest of the three, and it is often all a small site needs. A redesign goes further: the design is rebuilt from the brief, the page structure is rethought, the content is edited or rewritten, and the site is rebuilt page by page on the same platform or a similar one. It is the right choice when the design is dated, the structure no longer matches what the business offers, or the site does not work well on phones — but the platform and the content are still sound. A rebuild starts again: a new platform, new functionality, or a site whose content and structure both need to be made from scratch. It is the right choice when the platform itself is the problem, when the site needs features it cannot support, or when the content is so out of date that keeping it would cost more than rewriting it. A rebuild costs closest to a new website, because most of the work is new. Before asking for quotes, decide which of the three your site needs, and say so in the brief — a designer who quotes a refresh when you need a rebuild will look cheap and leave you with the same problems, and a designer who quotes a rebuild when you need a refresh will charge you for work you did not need. If you are unsure, ask a designer to look at the current site and tell you which one they recommend, and why. What Affects the Price: Six Factors

1. The number and type of pages. A five-page brochure site — home, about, services, gallery, contact — is a different job from a thirty-page site with service pages, location pages, a blog and a portfolio. Each page needs design, content, images and testing, and the price rises with the page count. The type of page matters too: a simple text page costs less than a page with a booking form, a gallery, a map, a price calculator or a shop, because those pages need functionality as well as design. Count the pages before you ask for a quote, and be honest about which ones need real work and which ones only need a tidy-up — a designer who knows the page list can quote accurately, and a designer who has to guess will either overcharge or come back for more later.

2. How custom the design is. A redesign built on a ready-made template, adapted with the business's colours, fonts and images, costs less than a design drawn from scratch for that one business. A template is a sensible choice for a small site with a straightforward job, and a good designer can make one look like the business rather than like the template. A custom design costs more because it is designed page by page, with the business's brand, its photography and its customers in mind — and it is worth paying for when the business needs to stand out, when the brand is distinctive, or when the site has pages a template cannot handle well. The question to ask is not "template or custom?" in the abstract, but "does this site need a design only it could have?" If the answer is no, a well-adapted template will serve the business well and save money for the content and the launch.

3. Content: who writes it, who photographs it. Content is the factor most quotes leave vague, and the one that causes most redesigns to run late. If the business supplies the text and the photos, the designer's job is design and build, and the price reflects that. If the designer has to write or rewrite the text, source or take the photos, and design the graphics, the price reflects a much larger job — and rightly so, because good content takes real time. Photography in particular moves the price: stock photos are cheap and often look generic, while real photos of the business, its team and its work cost more and do far more for trust. Be honest in the brief about what you can supply. If you can write the text and provide good photos, say so, and the quote will be lower. If you cannot, ask for content to be included in the quote, so the comparison between designers is fair. A quote that excludes content is not cheaper than one that includes it; it is simply incomplete.

4. Functionality: forms, booking, shop and extras. The features a site needs are the fourth factor, and they are where a simple redesign becomes a complex one. A contact form is standard. A booking system with calendars and payments is not. A small online shop with a handful of products is a different job from a shop with many products, variations, delivery rules and stock management. Member areas, customer logins, price calculators, multilingual versions and connections to other software — booking systems, customer records, email marketing — each add design, build and testing time, and each should be named in the brief. A designer who knows the features from the start can quote them properly and test them before launch. A designer who discovers them halfway through will either charge extra or leave them half-built. List every feature the current site has that must be kept, and every feature the new site must add, before asking for a price.

5. SEO and migration: protecting what the old site earned. An existing site has history: pages that search engines know, pages that rank, links from other sites, and a reputation built over years. A redesign that changes page addresses without redirecting them, removes the content that was ranking, or changes every page title without a plan can lose that history in a day. Protecting it takes a migration plan: a list of the pages that matter, redirects for any address that must change, the titles and content kept or carefully rewritten, and checks after launch. That plan takes time, and it should be part of the quote — ask every designer what their migration plan is, and be cautious of one who has none. The cheapest redesign is often the one that skips this work, and it is the most expensive in the long run, because recovering lost search visibility takes months. A redesign should leave the site ranking at least as well as before, and a proper migration plan is how that happens.

6. Who does the work, and what happens after launch. A freelancer, a small studio and a large agency charge differently, because they bring different things: a freelancer brings one person's skill and attention at a lower price; a studio brings a small team and a process; an agency brings a larger team, more specialisms and more overhead. None of those is automatically the right choice — it depends on the size and complexity of the site. What also matters is what happens after launch: whether the quote includes a period of fixes, training on how to update the site, and help if something breaks in the first weeks. A redesign that ends at launch, with no support and no training, will cost more in the first month than a quote that includes them. Ask what is included after launch, for how long, and what ongoing care costs — our guide to website maintenance costs covers the ongoing side in detail, so the redesign quote and the maintenance quote can be judged together.

General Market Ranges by Tier

The ranges below are what you will typically see across freelance marketplaces, studio pricing pages and agency portfolios. They are general market ranges, not quotes: the exact price for your site depends on the six factors above, and a designer will quote your job after looking at the site you already have. Treat these as a map, not a price list.

Light refresh, existing site kept: roughly a few hundred dollars to around $1,000–$2,000. New look on the same platform, tidied pages, new images and mobile fixes, with the content and structure mostly kept. This tier suits a small site whose content is good and whose only problem is that it looks dated. The work is mainly design and tidying, so it can be done quickly, and the risk to search visibility is low because the pages and addresses stay the same. Be clear about what a refresh does not include: it will not fix a broken structure, rewrite the content, or add new features.

Full redesign by a freelancer: roughly $1,000 to $3,000–$5,000. The design rebuilt from the brief, the pages rethought and rebuilt, the content edited, and the site tested on phones and in the main browsers. This is the tier where most small-business redesigns land, and it is usually the best value: a real person who looks at the existing site, designs for the business, and stays with the job through launch. The range is wide because a five-page site and a twenty-page site with a booking form are different jobs, even when both are done by one freelancer. Check the portfolio carefully at this tier, and look for redesigns of sites like yours, not just new builds.

Small studio redesign: roughly $3,000 to $8,000. A small team — a designer, a developer and often a copywriter — with a set process: brief, design drafts, build, content, testing and launch. The studio tier suits a business that needs custom design, rewritten content, or several features, and wants the reliability of a team rather than one person. You are paying for more thinking as well as more doing: the strategy behind the pages, the content written for the business, and a launch process that includes the migration plan. The posts and pages are usually more polished at this tier, because more than one specialist has worked on them.

Agency or large, complex redesign: roughly $8,000 and above. A larger team, custom design throughout, rewritten content, complex functionality — a shop, a booking system, a member area, multiple languages — and a formal migration and testing process. This tier suits a larger business, a site with many pages, or a site where the website is the main way customers buy or book. Hiring at this level is buying a full operation, not just a design, and the price reflects the team, the process and the risk management that a large site needs. A small business rarely needs this tier — but it is useful to know it exists, because it explains why the range is so wide: the top of the market is not buying pages, it is buying a system.

What You Get at Each Tier — and What Quotes Leave Out

The price difference between tiers is not just the number of pages. It is everything around them. At the refresh end, you get the new look, and you keep the rest: the content, the structure, the features, and the responsibility for updating the site. At the freelancer tier, you get the thinking as well: a design built for the business, content edited for the reader, and a site tested before launch. At the studio and agency tiers, you get the system: a strategy for the pages, content written for the business, a migration plan for the search history, and a launch process with checks. The right question when comparing quotes is therefore not "which redesign is cheapest?" but "which quote covers what my site actually needs?" A cheap quote that leaves out the migration plan is more expensive, in lost search traffic, than a proper quote that costs more. A full agency quote is wasted money if all the site needs is a refresh. Match the tier to the job, and read every quote for the same list: design, pages, content, features, migration, testing, launch, and what happens after launch. Anything missing from that list is either an extra charge waiting to happen or work that will not be done.

There are also costs that sit outside the redesign quote, and they should be budgeted for separately. Content is the first: copywriting, photography and graphics, if they are not included, will need to be paid for or supplied by the business. The second is the platform and the extras: hosting, the domain name, and any paid tools, themes or plugins the new site needs — some are one-off, some renew yearly, and the quote should say which is which. The third is ongoing care after launch: updates, backups, small changes and support, which our maintenance cost guide covers, so the redesign and the running costs can be planned together. The fourth is the time the business itself will spend: reviewing designs, supplying content, checking pages and giving feedback. That time is real, and a redesign that needs three rounds of feedback will take longer if the feedback takes weeks. None of these extras is a reason to avoid a redesign. They are reasons to ask for a quote that lists them, so the total cost is known before the work starts rather than discovered halfway through.

How to Get an Accurate Quote (and Compare Quotes Fairly)

The fastest way to get an accurate quote — and a good redesign — is a clear brief. Designers quote from the brief, so a vague brief produces a vague quote, and a vague quote produces surprises later. A good redesign brief answers seven questions. First, what is the current site's address, and what platform is it built on? Second, which tier of work do you think you need — refresh, redesign or rebuild — and why? Third, how many pages are there, and which ones matter most? Fourth, what features must be kept, and what features must be added? Fifth, who supplies the content — the text, the photos, the logo — and what needs to be created? Sixth, what is the migration plan you expect: which pages must keep their addresses, and what should happen to the ones that change? And seventh, what does success look like — more enquiries, more sales, a site that works on phones, or simply a site the business is proud to show? Send the same brief to every designer you contact, so the quotes you compare are for the same job. And ask every designer the same three questions before you decide: can I see redesigns you have done for sites like mine, what is your migration plan for a site that already ranks, and what is included after launch? The answers to those three questions are where cheap quotes usually hide their limits. Red Flags and Smart Ways to Save

A few warning signs are worth knowing before you pay anyone. Be cautious of a designer who will not look at the current site before quoting, because the quote cannot be accurate without it. Be cautious of a quote with no migration plan, because the site's search history is at risk. Be cautious of a portfolio that shows only new builds and no redesigns, because redesigning a live site is a different skill from building a new one. Be cautious of a price that includes no testing on phones, because most visitors will arrive on a phone. Be cautious of anyone who promises a set improvement in rankings or sales from the redesign alone — a redesign can help both, but no honest designer can promise a number. And be cautious of a quote that bundles everything together without saying what is design, what is content, and what is ongoing care. None of these mean the designer is dishonest — they usually mean the quote is smaller than it looks, and you should check before you buy. As for saving money without cutting corners: the best saving is to buy the right tier — a refresh when a refresh will do, rather than a rebuild. The second is to supply your own content where you can: good photos and honest text from the business cost the designer less time and make the site more real. The third is a clear brief, because it avoids revision rounds spent discovering what you wanted. And the fourth is timing: a redesign done in a quiet period, with the content ready before the build starts, will finish sooner and cost less in lost time than one started in the busiest month of the year.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a website redesign cost in 2026?
As a general market range, a light refresh of an existing small-business website typically runs from a few hundred dollars to around one or two thousand, a full redesign by a freelancer typically runs from around one thousand to a few thousand dollars, and an agency redesign typically starts at a few thousand dollars and rises with the number of pages, the custom design, and the functionality. The ranges are broad because the scope varies so much — a five-page brochure site and a large site with booking, shop or member features are different jobs. Always get a written quote for your exact scope rather than budgeting from a range.

Is it cheaper to redesign a website or build a new one?
A redesign is usually cheaper than building from scratch, because the content, the structure and the search history already exist and can be kept. A refresh that keeps the platform and the content, and changes the design, is the cheapest option. A redesign that also changes platform, rebuilds the functionality, or rewrites most of the content costs closer to a new build, because most of the work is new. The deciding factor is how much of the existing site is worth keeping: if the content is good and the platform suits the business, redesign it; if the platform is the problem, or the content needs rewriting throughout, a fresh build may be the honest choice.

How long does a website redesign take?
A small-business redesign typically takes a few weeks from a clear brief to launch, with the design and build taking most of that time and the client's feedback rounds taking the rest. A larger site with many pages, custom features, or rewritten content takes longer, because each of those adds review rounds. The most common cause of delay is not the build but the content — photos, text and approvals that arrive late. A redesign with the content ready before the build starts will finish noticeably sooner than one where the content is written as the pages are built.

Will a redesign hurt my Google rankings?
A redesign can hurt rankings if the page addresses change without redirects, the page titles change without a plan, or the content that was ranking is removed — and it can help rankings if the new site is faster, clearer and easier to use. The protection is a migration plan: keep the addresses of the pages that matter, redirect any that must change, keep the titles and the ranking content, and check the site after launch. Ask any designer you hire what their migration plan is before you agree to the work, and do not launch a redesign without one.
