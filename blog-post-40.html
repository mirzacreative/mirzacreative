How Much Does a Social Media Manager Cost in 2026?

November 20, 2026 • 10 min read • By Muhammad Atif Iqbal

Ask five businesses what they pay for social media management and you will get five different answers — from a few hundred dollars a month to several thousand. That spread is not a trick, and it is not a sign that someone is overcharging. It is a sign that "social media management" means very different things to different businesses. A freelancer posting three times a week on one platform is a different job from an agency running five platforms, creating short videos, replying to messages and managing paid ads. This guide explains what actually affects the price, the general market ranges you will see at each level, what you get at each tier, and how to hire a social media manager so the quote you receive matches the work you actually need. One thing to be clear about up front: the ranges in this post are general market ranges, not quotes and not a promise of any price. Your exact price depends on your scope, and any manager worth hiring will quote your job in writing after reading your brief.

The reason this question comes up so often is that social media is usually the first marketing a small business buys — and it is the piece the business has to keep paying for, month after month, to see results. A website is built once; social media has to be fed every week. A business that hires the cheapest option and gets three generic posts a week will wonder why nothing changes. A business that hires well gets a consistent presence, content its customers actually want, replies to the people who comment, and a monthly report that shows what is working. So the useful question is not "what is the cheapest social media manager?" — it is "what should I pay for the social media management my business actually needs?" The rest of this guide answers that question, starting with the six things that move the price.

What Affects the Price: Six Factors

1. The number of platforms. Managing one platform is a different job from managing five. Each platform has its own formats, its own posting rhythm and its own audience: Instagram wants reels and carousels, TikTok wants short vertical video, LinkedIn wants written posts, Facebook wants a mix of both. A manager who runs two platforms can reuse the same idea across both with different formats; a manager who runs five is doing five different jobs. The price rises with each platform, and it should — so start with the one or two platforms where your customers actually are, rather than paying to be everywhere at once.

2. Posting frequency. Three posts a week costs less than daily posting, and daily posting costs less than posting twice a day. But frequency is not just the number of posts — it is the work behind each one. A post that is a stock photo with a caption takes minutes; a post that is a designed graphic, a short video and a caption written for your brand takes far longer. When you compare quotes, compare the posting frequency and the content quality together: a quote for daily posting with generic content is not the same job as a quote for three posts a week with properly designed, original content.

3. Content creation. Who creates the content is the biggest single difference between cheap and expensive packages. At the low end, the manager schedules posts you provide — your photos, your captions, your ideas. In the middle, the manager creates the graphics, writes the captions and plans the content calendar, using photos and material you supply. At the top end, the manager also shoots the photos and videos, edits the reels, writes the scripts and designs the templates. Video in particular moves the price: a package that includes two edited reels a week costs noticeably more than a package of static graphics, because each reel is a small production. Be honest about what you can supply yourself — if you can take good photos of your products, a manager who designs around them will cost less than one who has to create everything from scratch.

4. Community management and replies. Some packages cover only posting; others include replying to comments and direct messages, and that difference matters more than most businesses expect. A comment left unanswered for a week is a customer ignored in public, and a direct message left unread is a sale lost. A package that includes daily replies — answering questions, thanking customers, handling complaints politely — costs more, because it means the manager is working for you every day, not just on posting days. Ask every manager you contact whether replies are included, how quickly they reply, and whether they handle complaints or pass them to you. A manager who replies well is worth paying for; a manager who posts and disappears is not.

5. Paid ads. Managing paid social media ads is a separate skill from posting, and it is usually priced separately — either as a higher monthly fee or as a percentage of the ad spend. A package that includes ads should cover the ad creative, the audience targeting, the budget management and the reporting, and the ad spend itself should always be separate from the management fee: you should know exactly how much went to the platform and how much went to the manager. Be cautious of a quote that bundles the two together without saying how much is which. And remember that ad management only makes sense once the organic content is working — running ads to a profile with three posts and no followers is spending money to send people to an empty shop.

6. Experience and reporting. A manager with a track record — businesses they have grown, content they have made, results they can show — charges more than a beginner, and the difference is usually visible in the first month: better content, better captions, better timing, and a report that actually tells you something. Reporting is the part most cheap packages skip, and it is the part that tells you whether the money is working. A proper monthly report shows what was posted, how each post performed, what the audience did, and what will change next month. If a manager cannot show you a sample report, or will not commit to one monthly, that is a warning sign — you will be paying every month without knowing what the money bought.

General Market Ranges by Tier

The ranges below are what you will typically see across freelance marketplaces, agency pricing pages and manager portfolios. They are general market ranges, not quotes: the exact price for your business depends on the six factors above, and a manager will quote your job after reading your brief. Treat these as a map, not a price list.

Do-it-yourself with scheduling tools: roughly $0 to $100 a month. Scheduling tools let you plan and post your own content from one dashboard, and some offer basic design templates. The cost is low because the work is yours: you write the captions, you make the graphics, you reply to the comments. This tier can work for a business owner who enjoys social media and has a few hours a week to spend on it. The trade-off is time — and consistency, because the posts stop when the owner gets busy, which is usually the week the business needs them most.

Part-time freelancer, one or two platforms: roughly $300 to $1,000 a month. At this level you are usually working with a freelancer who manages one or two platforms, posts a few times a week, creates the graphics and captions, and handles the basics of replies. The quality varies at this tier, so check the portfolio carefully: look at the accounts they already manage, read the captions, and check whether the content is original or generic. This is the tier where most small businesses land, and it is usually the best value — a real person who knows your business, posting consistently, for a price a small business can afford.

Full-service freelancer: roughly $1,000 to $3,000 a month. This is the tier where the manager takes the whole job off your hands: several platforms, daily or near-daily posting, original content including short video, community management with daily replies, and a monthly report. The manager will plan the content calendar, create the graphics and reels, write the captions, reply to customers, and tell you each month what worked and what will change. For a business that relies on social media for enquiries and sales, this is usually the right level — the social media is actually being managed, not just posted.

Small agency: roughly $2,000 to $5,000 a month. An agency brings a team: a strategist who plans the content, a designer who makes the graphics, a copywriter who writes the captions, and often an ads specialist who manages the paid campaigns. You are paying for more thinking as well as more doing, and for the reliability of a team rather than one person — the posts do not stop when one person is on holiday. This tier suits a business that needs several platforms managed to a high standard, with paid ads, and wants the strategy as well as the execution.

Large agency or in-house hire: roughly $5,000 a month and above, or a full-time salary. At this level the social media is a full operation: a dedicated team or a full-time employee, daily content across many platforms, video production, influencer work, and paid campaigns with a serious budget. Hiring in-house usually means a salary in the general range of a marketing employee, plus the tools and the ad spend on top. A small business rarely needs this tier — but it is useful to know it exists, because it explains why the range is so wide: the top of the market is not buying posts, it is buying a marketing department.

What You Get at Each Tier

The price difference between tiers is not just the number of posts — it is everything around them. At the budget end, you get the posts, and you manage the rest yourself: you supply the photos, you answer the messages, you decide what to post next. At the freelancer tier, you get the thinking as well: a content calendar, captions written for your brand, graphics designed for your business, and replies to the people who comment. At the agency tier, you get the system: a strategy that explains why the content looks the way it does, a team that keeps it running, and reporting that shows whether it is working. The right question when comparing quotes is therefore not "which manager is cheapest?" but "which package covers what I actually need?" A cheap package that posts three generic graphics a week and never replies to a comment is more expensive, in lost customers, than a proper package that costs twice as much. And a full agency package is wasted money if all you need is three good posts a week on Instagram. Match the tier to the job: a side project can live with a scheduling tool, a growing business usually needs a freelancer, and a business that depends on social media for sales deserves a full-service manager or an agency. How to Hire a Social Media Manager (and Get an Accurate Quote)

The fastest way to get an accurate quote — and good social media — is a clear brief. Managers quote from the brief, so a vague brief produces a vague quote, and a vague quote produces surprises later. A good brief answers six questions. First, which platforms, and why those ones? Name the platforms where your customers actually are, not the ones you think you should be on. Second, how often should each platform be posted to, and what kind of content — static graphics, carousels, reels, stories? Third, who creates the content: do you supply photos and videos, or does the manager create everything? Fourth, who replies to comments and messages, and how quickly? Fifth, are paid ads included, and is the ad spend separate from the fee? And sixth, what does success look like — more enquiries, more sales, more followers, or simply a consistent professional presence? Send the same brief to every manager you contact, so the quotes you compare are for the same job. And ask every manager the same three questions before you decide: can I see accounts you currently manage, what does your monthly report look like, and what happens when you are on holiday? The answers to those three questions are where cheap quotes usually hide their limits. Red Flags and Smart Ways to Save

A few warning signs are worth knowing before you pay anyone. Be cautious of a manager who will not show accounts they currently manage, or whose portfolio is all work in one style — your content should be built for your business, not pulled from a template. Be cautious of a price that includes no reporting, because you will be paying every month without knowing what the money bought. Be cautious of anyone who promises a set number of followers in a set time — followers can be bought, and bought followers do not buy anything. Be cautious of a manager who posts and never replies to comments, because the replies are where the customers are. And be cautious of a quote that bundles ad spend and management fees together without saying how much is which. None of these mean the manager is dishonest — they usually mean the package is smaller than it looks, and you should check before you buy. As for saving money without cutting corners: the best saving is to start with one platform, managed well, rather than paying to be everywhere badly. The second is to supply your own photos and videos — a manager who designs around your material costs less than one who has to create everything. The third is a clear brief, because it avoids revision rounds spent discovering what you wanted. And the fourth is a realistic timeline: social media grows over months, not days, and a manager who promises overnight results is selling a promise they cannot keep.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a social media manager cost in 2026?
As a general market range, a part-time freelancer managing one or two platforms typically costs a few hundred dollars a month, a full-service freelancer managing several platforms with content creation typically runs from around one thousand to a few thousand dollars a month, and an agency typically starts at a few thousand dollars a month and rises with the scope. The ranges are broad because the scope varies so much — the number of platforms, the posting frequency, whether content is created from scratch, and whether paid ads are managed. Always get a written quote for your exact scope rather than budgeting from a range.

Is it cheaper to hire a freelancer or an agency for social media?
A freelancer is usually cheaper, because you are paying one person rather than a team, and a freelancer is often the better choice for a small business that needs one or two platforms managed well. An agency costs more, but it brings a team — a strategist, a designer, a copywriter, an ads specialist — and the reliability of a team rather than one person. The right choice depends on the scope: if you need daily posting across five platforms plus paid ads, an agency's team may be worth the price; if you need three posts a week on Instagram and Facebook, a good freelancer will usually do the job for less.

What should a social media management package include?
A proper package should state the platforms covered, the number of posts per week, who creates the content — the graphics, the captions, the short videos — and who replies to comments and messages. It should include a monthly report showing what was posted, how it performed, and what will change next month. If paid ads are included, the ad spend should be separate from the management fee, and the reporting should show the ad spend and the results separately. Anything that is not written down — the posting frequency, the content types, the response time for messages — is a disagreement waiting to happen.

Can I hire a social media manager for one platform only?
Yes, and for a small business it is often the smartest way to start. Managing one platform well — posting consistently, creating content your audience actually wants, replying to comments — will do more for a business than managing five platforms badly. Many freelancers offer a single-platform package, and it is the cheapest way to find out whether professional social media management works for your business before you commit to a larger scope.
