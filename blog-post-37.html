How Much Does a Website Cost to Maintain in 2026?

November 17, 2026 • 7 min read • By Muhammad Atif Iqbal

Most people budget carefully for building a website — and then forget that it costs money every year after that. A website is not a poster you hang on a wall and forget about. It is a living thing: software that needs updates, a domain that needs renewing, a host that needs paying, and content that slowly goes out of date. Ignore those costs and the site will quietly break — usually at the worst possible moment. This guide breaks down what website maintenance actually costs in 2026, line by line, so you can budget for it honestly and spot a maintenance plan that is fair value.

One note before the numbers: every figure below is a typical range, not a fixed price. Your costs depend on the size of your site, the platform it runs on, and who looks after it. Treat the ranges as a way to build a realistic annual budget and to judge whether a quote is fair — not as a price list. Where a cost is usually included in something else, the guide says so.

The Domain Name: Small, But Non-Negotiable

Your domain name — the address people type to reach your site — is rented, not owned, and the rent comes due every year. A standard domain typically costs in the region of $10 to $20 a year to renew, depending on the extension: common extensions like .com sit in that range, while some newer or country-specific extensions cost more. The domain is the cheapest item on this list and the most dangerous to forget, because if it expires, your website and your email can stop working on the same day, and someone else can register the name while you are sorting it out. Renew it for more than one year if you can, and make sure the renewal emails go to an inbox somebody actually checks. Domain privacy protection, which hides your personal details from the public register, is often included free by registrars now, and where it is charged it usually costs only a few dollars a year. This is a small cost — budget for it, automate the renewal, and never let it lapse.

Hosting: The Biggest Recurring Cost

Hosting is the rent for the server your website lives on, and it is usually the largest recurring cost for a small site. Shared hosting — where your site shares a server with many others — typically costs a few dollars a month, often in the range of $3 to $15 a month for a small business site, depending on the plan and the provider. That usually works out to roughly $40 to $180 a year. Managed hosting, where the provider handles updates, backups and security for you, costs more — typically in the range of $20 to $50 a month for a small site — but it removes much of the maintenance work from your plate. An online shop, or a site with heavy traffic, needs a stronger plan and costs more again. Watch out for the first-year discount trap: many hosts advertise a low first-year price and renew at two or three times that amount, so always check the renewal price before you commit. The honest question is not "what is the cheapest hosting?" but "what does the hosting cost in year two, and what does it include?" Backups and an SSL certificate are often included in the plan — check before you pay for them separately.

SSL: Usually Free, Sometimes Not

An SSL certificate is what puts the padlock in the browser address bar and the "https" at the start of your web address. It encrypts the connection between your visitors and your site, and browsers now warn visitors away from sites that do not have one. In 2026, most hosting plans include a free SSL certificate, and for most small business sites that free certificate is all you need. If your host charges separately, a basic certificate typically costs in the region of $10 to $100 a year. The main cost here is not money but attention: a certificate that expires without being renewed will show your visitors a security warning, which is worse than having no certificate at all. If your host manages the certificate automatically, that is one less thing to worry about; if it does not, put the expiry date in your calendar with a reminder a month ahead.

Updates and Security: The Work You Cannot Skip

Software updates are the core of website maintenance, and they are also the part most owners skip. Your site runs on software — a content management system, a theme, plugins, or a builder platform — and every one of those gets security updates. An update is usually a small job: apply it, check the site still works, and move on. The cost is time, or a maintenance plan that does it for you. If you do it yourself, budget an hour or two a month. If a freelancer or agency does it, updates are usually part of a monthly care plan, typically in the range of $30 to $100 a month for a small site, depending on what else the plan includes. Security monitoring — a firewall, malware scanning, and login protection — is often included in that plan, or available as an add-on for a modest monthly fee. The cost of skipping updates is much higher: a hacked site needs cleaning, and a hacked site that Google flags will lose its visitors until it is fixed. Small, regular updates are cheap. Emergency repairs are not.

Backups: Cheap Insurance

A backup is a copy of your website that you can restore if something goes wrong — a bad update, a hack, or a hosting failure. Many hosts include automatic daily backups in the plan, and for a small site that is usually enough. If your host does not include backups, a backup plugin or service typically costs a few dollars a month, or a small one-off fee. The important thing is not the cost but the habit: check that backups are actually running, and check that you can restore one. A backup that has never been tested is a hope, not a plan. Keep at least one copy somewhere other than your hosting account — your own computer or cloud storage — so a problem with the host does not take your backups with it. For most small sites, backups are one of the cheapest items in the maintenance budget and one of the most valuable when something goes wrong.

Content Updates and Small Fixes

Websites need small changes all year: a new team member on the about page, a price change, a seasonal offer, a broken link, a photo that needs replacing. If you make these changes yourself, the cost is your time. If someone else makes them, budget for a few hours a year at your provider's hourly rate, or check whether your maintenance plan includes a monthly allowance of small edits — many do, and that allowance is often the best value in the plan. Content also goes stale: a blog post from two years ago with old prices, a services page that lists something you no longer offer, a testimonial from a client you no longer work with. Updating content is not a technical job, but it is a real one, and a site with stale content looks neglected to visitors even when it works perfectly. Set aside an hour every couple of months to read your own site as a customer would, and fix what is out of date. A site that is current earns trust; a site that is stale quietly loses it.

What It Adds Up To: A Realistic Annual Budget

A simple small business website. Domain renewal, basic hosting, a free SSL certificate, and updates and backups handled either by you or by a modest care plan. Doing the maintenance yourself, the cash cost is typically in the region of $60 to $200 a year — mostly hosting and the domain. With a care plan that covers updates, backups, security monitoring and small edits, the total is typically in the region of $400 to $1,200 a year, depending on the plan and the provider.

An online shop. Everything above, plus the shop platform's fees, payment processing, and more frequent updates as products change. The hosting needs to be stronger, the backups more frequent, and the content changes more regular. A small shop typically costs several times a simple site to maintain each year, and the product count is the biggest driver — every product is a page that can go out of date.

A site you ignore. The cash cost is almost nothing in the short term, and the real cost arrives later: an expired domain, a hacked site, a broken contact form nobody noticed, or a rebuild because the software is too old to update safely. That bill is almost always larger than a year of maintenance would have been.

DIY Maintenance vs a Care Plan

Doing it yourself costs the least in cash and the most in attention. It works well if you are comfortable with the platform, you remember to do the updates, and your site is simple. It works badly if you are busy — and most business owners are — because maintenance is the first thing that gets postponed and the last thing that gets done.

A care plan from your web provider or a freelancer usually covers updates, backups, security monitoring and a monthly allowance of small edits, for a fixed monthly fee. It makes sense when the website matters to your revenue, when you cannot afford downtime, or when you simply want someone else to worry about it. Before you sign up, ask exactly what is included: how often are updates applied, how often are backups taken, what happens if the site goes down, and how many small edits are included each month. A plan that answers those questions clearly is usually a plan that delivers.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to maintain a small business website per year?
For a simple small business website, typical annual maintenance costs — domain, hosting, security updates and small fixes — usually fall in the low hundreds of dollars a year. An online shop or a site with regular content changes costs more, because it needs more updates, backups and support.

Do I have to pay for a website every year?
Yes. A website has recurring annual costs: the domain name must be renewed every year, hosting is paid monthly or yearly, and the site needs security updates and occasional fixes. A site that is never updated will slowly break as software, browsers and security standards change.

Is a website maintenance plan worth it?
For most small businesses, yes — if the plan covers updates, backups, security monitoring and small edits. It is cheaper than paying for emergency fixes, and it means the site is looked after instead of being forgotten until something breaks. Check exactly what the plan includes before you sign up.

What happens if I stop maintaining my website?
The site will keep working for a while, but it gradually becomes outdated and vulnerable: software goes out of date, security holes appear, links break, and the content goes stale. Eventually something breaks — often at the worst moment — and the repair usually costs more than the maintenance would have.
