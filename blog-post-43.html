FLYER DESIGN PRICING

How Much Does Flyer Design Cost in 2026?

November 24, 2026 • 13 min read • By Muhammad Atif Iqbal

A flyer is the fastest piece of print design most businesses will ever buy, and that speed is exactly why its price is so often misunderstood. A flyer is handed over in seconds, glanced at for a moment, and kept or discarded on the strength of that glance, and the whole job can look, from the outside, like arranging a headline, a photograph and a date on a single page. In practice, a good flyer is a piece of persuasion that has to work at arm's length, in a busy street, on a crowded noticeboard, or in a pile of post arriving through a door, and it has to do all of that with one message, one offer and one next step, set large enough to be read before the reader's attention moves on. The design fee pays for the judgement that makes that single page work: what the headline must say, what the reader must notice first, second and third, how much the flyer can say before it says too much, and how the finished artwork must be prepared so that the printer returns the flyer the designer intended, with nothing cut off, no fold in the wrong place, and no colour surprise. This guide explains what flyer design cost actually covers in 2026, the general market ranges for design alone, how flyer sizes, folds and the choice between one side and two sides move the price, what a proper flyer design package includes, and how design, printing and distribution interact, so that the total cost of a finished, delivered flyer is clear before anyone is paid. One thing to be clear about up front: the ranges in this post are general market ranges, not quotes and not a promise of any price. Your exact price depends on your flyer, your size, your folds and your printer, and any designer worth hiring will quote your job in writing after reading your brief.

The reason the question matters is that a flyer is still, for many local businesses, events and services, the cheapest way to reach people who are not looking online. A website waits to be found, and a social media post waits to be shown, but a flyer is placed where the reader already is: in a shop window, on a community board, in a hand at a market, or through the door of a home in the area the business serves. That directness is what makes the design stage worth doing properly, even though the flyer is a single page. If the headline is unclear, if the date or the address is hard to find, if the offer is buried under too many words, or if the flyer looks crowded and cheap beside the flyers it will sit next to, the business lives with that impression for the whole print run, because a printed flyer cannot be edited after it leaves the printer. That permanence, combined with the short time a flyer gets to make its point, is what makes the cheapest route risky: a flyer assembled in a hurry from a generic template, with no check of the print settings, can look acceptable on a screen and disappointing in the hand, because a screen hides the very things print reveals — small text that cannot be read at arm's length, a photograph that breaks up when printed large, colour that shifts on paper, and a fold that lands across a headline. The useful question is therefore not "what is the cheapest flyer?" but "what should I pay for a flyer design that will be noticed, read, kept, and acted on, and printed well at the quantity I need?" The rest of this guide answers that question, starting with what flyer design actually is, and what it is not.

What Flyer Design Actually Is

Flyer design is the layout of a single printed page — or a single folded sheet — so that a stranger can understand it in seconds, remember the one thing it is asking them to do, and know how to do it. The designer decides the hierarchy: which piece of information is read first, which second, and which can wait until the reader turns the flyer over or opens it. In most cases the headline comes first, because it is what stops the reader: the event, the offer, the problem the service solves, or the benefit the reader will get, stated in a few words that can be read from a distance. The supporting detail comes next: the date, the time, the place, the price or the offer, and the single next step — call, book, visit, scan, or order — set large enough to act on without hunting. Everything else is supporting material: a photograph or illustration that helps the flyer be recognised again, a short line about the business, a code that links to a booking page, or the small print that must be there for honesty, such as terms, age limits, or opening hours. A designer who understands hierarchy will resist the temptation to fill every corner. Empty space on a flyer is not wasted space. It is what lets the eye find the headline, and it is what keeps the flyer from looking like a poster that has been shrunk until it is unreadable. A flyer that tries to say everything will be read by nobody, because the reader will conclude, in the first second, that reading it would take too long, and will move on.

What flyer design is not is a new brand identity, and it is not a website. A flyer design normally works with artwork and colours that already exist: the business name, its existing mark, its colours and its fonts, supplied by the client or taken from an earlier brand package. If the business does not yet have those things, the flyer job becomes larger, because the designer must first settle the colours, the fonts and the arrangement that the flyer will then use — and that wider work is priced separately, as our companion post on how much a logo costs in 2026 explains for the identity side of the work. A flyer is also not a website in miniature. A website can carry pages of detail, because a visitor who has arrived at a website has already decided to look. A flyer must carry one message, because its reader has decided nothing yet. That distinction matters when comparing quotes and when writing the brief. One quote may assume the client will supply the artwork, the wording and the photographs, ready to lay out, while another may include time to tidy the artwork, choose matching fonts, source or improve a photograph, or shorten the wording so that it fits the page. Both can be fair quotes, but they are quotes for different jobs, and the cheaper one is not cheaper if the wording still needs to be written or the photograph still needs to be found before the flyer can be laid out. Ask, before you accept any quote, what the designer expects you to supply, and what the designer will prepare. The answer will tell you whether the price covers the flyer alone, or the flyer plus the preparation that makes the flyer possible.

Flyer, Leaflet, Poster: Knowing Which One You Need

The words flyer, leaflet and poster are often used as if they meant the same thing, and the confusion costs money, because each one is a different job with a different price. A flyer is a single, usually unfolded sheet, printed on one or both sides, meant to be handed out, posted, or left in a stack for people to take. It carries one message and one next step, and it is read in seconds. A leaflet is a folded sheet, usually with several panels, meant to be read in order, like a small booklet. It carries more information than a flyer, because the reader who opens a leaflet has already decided to read further, and it suits a business that needs to explain a service, a menu, a set of prices, or a sequence of steps. A poster is a larger single sheet, meant to be seen from a distance on a wall or in a window, and it carries even less than a flyer: a headline, an image, a date and a next step, set very large, because it will be read from several metres away. The three are priced differently because they are designed differently. A flyer is one or two layouts. A leaflet is several panels that must work in order and fold correctly. A poster is one layout at a larger size, where the headline, the image and the spacing must work at a distance. Before asking for a quote, decide which of the three you need, by asking how the reader will meet it. If it will be handed to people, posted through doors, or left in a stack, it is a flyer. If it will be picked up and read in order, with several things to explain, it is a leaflet. If it will be fixed to a wall or a window and read from across a room, it is a poster. A designer who is told the wrong format will design a page that looks striking and fails in use: a poster crammed with flyer detail that cannot be read from a distance, or a flyer with poster-sized emptiness that wastes the one page it has. The format should follow the reading situation, not fashion, and the quote should follow the format.

Flyer Sizes: A5, A4, DL and Square

The most common flyer sizes are A5, A4 and DL, and each suits a different job. A5, which is half of an A4 sheet, is the standard handout flyer: large enough to carry a headline, a photograph, the key details and a next step, and small enough to be held, pocketed, or posted through a door without folding. It is the safe, practical choice for most flyers, because printers stock paper and cutting tools for it, it fits the display stands and holders that businesses use, and it costs the standard print price. A4, the full standard sheet, suits a flyer that must carry more: a menu, a price list, a programme, or a flyer that will be pinned to a board and read from a step back. It gives the designer room for a larger photograph and more detail, but it also gives the wording room to grow, and a flyer that fills an A4 page with text has usually stopped being a flyer and become a leaflet that forgot to fold. DL, a tall, narrow size — a third of an A4 sheet — suits a flyer that will be racked, posted, or slipped into an envelope: a takeaway menu, a service card, a voucher, or a flyer for a rack in a hotel, a clinic or a shop. Its narrow shape suits a short message and a single image, and it forces the wording to be brief, which is usually a virtue. Square flyers, and other less standard sizes, stand out in a stack of rectangles, and they suit a business that wants a modern look, a photograph-led design, or a flyer that will also be shared as a square image online. They hold less room for a long line of text on a single line, however, so the wording must be planned for the shape, and the print cost can be higher, because the size may not match the printer's standard cutting. Before choosing a size, check which sizes your printer stocks, how the flyer will be distributed, and how it will be read. A flyer that will be posted through doors should fit through a door and a letterbox without folding. A flyer that will be handed out at an event should fit in a hand and a pocket. A flyer that will be pinned to a board should be readable from the distance at which it will be read. Print one flyer at the intended size, hold it at the distance at which it will be read, and check that the headline is readable and the next step is findable. That test, done once, will settle the size better than any discussion on a screen.

Size also affects the design work, and therefore the price, in a way that is easy to overlook. A larger flyer is not simply a smaller flyer made bigger. A photograph that looks sharp at A5 may look soft at A4, because it is being printed larger from the same file, and the designer must check the resolution at the true size before the layout is approved. A headline set for A5 will need to be reset for DL, because the narrow shape changes how the words break across lines. A flyer that must work at two sizes — a printed A5 flyer and a square image for social media, for example — is two layouts, not one, even when the wording is the same, because the shape, the hierarchy and the image crop must all be reworked for the second size. That second version is often worth paying for, because a flyer that is designed once and shared everywhere will usually look wrong somewhere: cropped, cramped, or unreadable at the size where it is actually seen. Ask, before the work starts, whether the quote covers one size or several, and whether a digital version for sharing is included. A quote that covers one size and leaves the digital version to be made later, by someone else, from a screenshot of the print file, will produce a digital flyer that looks like a photograph of a flyer, which is what it will be. A package that includes the print file and a properly made screen version, each designed for its own size, is a fuller package, and it is worth comparing quotes on that basis, size by size.

Single-Sided, Double-Sided and Folded: What Each Side Must Do

A single-sided flyer carries everything on one face and leaves the back blank, or printed in a plain colour. A double-sided flyer uses both faces. A folded flyer uses several panels. The choice is not a matter of getting more for the money. It is a matter of what the flyer must do, and how much the reader should be asked to do. A single-sided flyer is the clearest flyer there is: everything the reader needs is in front of them, there is nothing to turn over, and the blank back is a useful surface — a place to write a note, a date, or a reminder, or a surface that keeps the printing cost down. That matters for a flyer that will be handed out quickly, read at a glance, and acted on at once: an event flyer, a special-offer flyer, or a flyer for a single service. A double-sided flyer, by contrast, is the right choice when the front alone cannot carry what the flyer must say without becoming crowded. The usual arrangement is a front that carries the headline, the image, and the single next step, and a back that carries the rest: the full details, the prices, the map or directions, the terms, or the second message for a different reader. What the back should not do is repeat the front. A back that repeats the headline, the date and the phone number in the same arrangement as the front adds nothing, and it wastes the one extra surface the flyer has. Designing a double-sided flyer costs more than designing a single-sided one, because the back is a second layout that must be balanced, aligned with the front, and proofed separately, even when the back is simpler than the front. Printing it also usually costs more, because both sides must be printed and registered so that the two sides line up when the sheet is cut.

A folded flyer — a leaflet — is a different job again, and it should only be chosen when the message genuinely needs panels. The common folds are the bi-fold, a single fold making four panels, and the tri-fold, two folds making six panels, and each fold changes how the reader meets the information. The front panel is the cover: it must carry the headline and the reason to open the flyer, and nothing else. The inside panels carry the detail, in the order the reader will open them, and the back panel carries the contact details, the map, or the next step, because it is the panel the reader sees last. A fold that lands across a headline, a photograph, or a line of text will break the reading, and a panel that is read out of order will confuse the reader, so the designer must plan the fold from the start, with the printer's fold specification agreed before the artwork is finished. Folded flyers cost more to design, because each panel is a layout and the panels must work together, and more to print, because folding is a finishing step that must be checked. They are justified when the flyer must explain something: a service with several steps, a menu with several sections, a programme with a timetable, or a business with several offers that cannot fit on one page without crowding. They are not justified when the message is one offer, one event, or one service, because a reader who must open a flyer to learn the date will often not open it at all. Before you ask for a quote, write down what each side, or each panel, must do in one sentence. If you cannot write that sentence for the back, the flyer probably only needs one side. If you cannot write it for the inside panels, the flyer probably does not need to fold. The money saved on a second layout, or on a fold, can go to better paper, a larger print run, or a second version for sharing online, where it will do more good.

Paper, Finishes and the Feel of the Flyer

The paper a flyer is printed on is part of the design, not an afterthought, because the reader feels the flyer before reading it, and because the paper decides how the flyer survives being handled, posted, or left in a pile. Flyers are usually printed on lighter paper than business cards, because they are produced in larger quantities and must be economical to print and post, but the paper should still feel deliberate. A very thin flyer can feel flimsy beside the flyers it will sit next to, can show the print through from the other side, and can tear or crease in a pocket. A very thick flyer can feel impressive, but it can also be expensive to print in quantity and heavy to post, and it can be mistaken for a card rather than a flyer. The sensible approach is to handle samples before committing to a print run. Ask the printer for samples of the papers and finishes you are considering, at the flyer size you intend to use, and compare them in your hand, not only on a screen. A finish that looks subtle in a photograph can feel striking in the hand, and a finish that looks striking in a photograph can feel slippery or hard to read in real light. The designer should know the paper and finish before the final artwork is made, because some finishes change how colour and fine detail appear, and the artwork must be prepared for the finish that will actually be used. A flyer that will be posted through doors, left outside, or handled in wet weather may need a coated paper that resists moisture better than an uncoated one. A flyer that will be written on — a voucher, an appointment card, or a flyer with a form to complete — needs an uncoated surface that takes a pen. Tell the designer and the printer how the flyer will be used, before the paper is chosen, because the use decides the paper, and the paper decides part of the price.

The common finishes each do a different job, and the choice should follow the flyer's purpose rather than fashion. An uncoated, matte paper has no shine, feels like good writing paper, takes a pen easily, and suits a business that wants a quiet, natural, or craft feel — a clinic, a consultancy, a maker, a community group. A gloss or silk-coated paper has a sheen, makes photographs look richer, and resists marks and moisture better than uncoated paper, but it is harder to write on and can glare under bright light. A folded flyer on a heavier coated paper will fold less cleanly than the same flyer on a lighter paper, because the paper cracks at the fold, so the fold and the paper must be chosen together, and the printer should advise on the combination. Special effects — foil, embossing, spot gloss — are rarely used on flyers, because a flyer is produced in quantity and must be economical, and because an effect that is striking on a single card can look wasteful on a thousand flyers. Where a special effect is wanted, it should be one effect, used sparingly, on the element that matters most, and it will need its own artwork layer and a production check, which add to the cost. A flyer with a foil headline and a quiet layout will be remembered. A flyer with several effects will be remembered for the wrong reason, and will cost more per flyer than the message justifies. The most reliable way to make a flyer feel premium, without a special effect, is to choose a good paper, a clean layout, and a photograph that is sharp at the true size. Those three, together, will do more for a flyer than any finish applied to a crowded layout.

Design Cost Versus Printing and Distribution: Three Separate Bills

The most common confusion in flyer pricing is the belief that there is one price for a flyer. In practice there are almost always three: the price of designing the flyer, the price of printing it, and the price of getting it to the reader. The design fee is usually a one-off payment. It pays for the designer's time: understanding the brief, arranging the content, choosing and setting the type, placing the artwork and the photographs, preparing the print-ready files, and making the agreed revision rounds. Once the design is approved and the files are delivered, the design fee is done, and a reprint of the same flyer should not need a new design fee, unless the content changes. The printing fee is a per-run payment. It pays for the paper, the ink, the finish, the cutting and folding, and the labour of producing the number of flyers ordered, and it is paid again every time a new batch is printed. The distribution cost is the third bill, and it is the one most often forgotten: the postage for a door-drop or a mailing, the cost of a delivery service, the time spent handing flyers out or placing them in shops and racks, and the cost of the envelopes or the holders they will sit in. Some printers bundle a basic layout service with the print order, usually from a template library, and some designers include a first print run in their package price. Both arrangements are legitimate, but they must be read carefully. A bundled layout is not the same as a custom design, and a package that includes printing is only good value if the paper, finish and quantity it includes are the ones the business actually wants. Always ask, in plain words: does this price include the design, the printing, the distribution, or all three, and if it includes printing, how many flyers, on what paper, with what finish and what fold?

Separating the three bills also makes the long-term cost clearer. A custom design that costs more up front can be the cheaper choice over several print runs, because the design is paid for once and the reprints cost only the printing and the distribution. A cheap template layout that is tied to one printer, in one file format that cannot be taken elsewhere, can become expensive when the business changes printer, changes a date or a price, or wants the same flyer in a different size, because the layout must then be remade. Distribution deserves the same care. A thousand flyers that sit in a box because nobody was paid or given time to deliver them are not cheap flyers. They are wasted flyers, and the money spent printing them would have been better spent on a smaller run, properly delivered, and a second, updated run later. When you order the first run, ask how a reprint will work, what file will be used, and whether a small change — a new date, a new price, a new phone number — will be charged as a small edit or as a new design. The answer is part of the true cost of the flyer, even though it will not be paid until later. And when you compare quotes, compare design with design, printing with printing, and distribution with distribution. A quote that looks expensive because it includes a thousand printed flyers is not comparable with a quote that prices the design alone. Put the three parts side by side, in the same table, with the same quantity, the same paper and the same finish, and the comparison will be honest. If a quote does not separate the three parts, ask for the separation. A designer or printer who will not separate them is asking you to buy without knowing what you are buying.

How Quantity Changes the Total

Printing quantity changes the total in a way that surprises many first-time buyers, because the relationship is not a straight line. The design fee does not change with quantity: designing a flyer that will be printed one hundred times costs the same as designing a flyer that will be printed five thousand times, because the layout is the same. The printing fee, however, falls sharply per flyer as the quantity rises, because the fixed costs of setting up the print job — preparing the file, setting the paper, checking the first sheets, setting the cutter and the folder — are spread across more flyers. The first hundred flyers therefore carry a high cost per flyer, the next few hundred carry a much lower one, and by the time the run reaches a few thousand, the cost per flyer is often a small fraction of what it was for the first hundred. That is why printers quote in bands — commonly one hundred, two hundred and fifty, five hundred, one thousand, and two thousand five hundred flyers — and why the jump from one band to the next can look like very good value. It is good value only if the flyers will be used. A thousand flyers that sit in a drawer because the date passed, the price changed, or the offer ended are not cheap flyers. They are wasted flyers, and the money spent printing them would have been better spent on a smaller run and a second, updated run later. The sensible quantity is therefore a judgement, not a calculation. A flyer for a one-off event, with a fixed date, should be printed in the quantity that can be distributed before the event, with a small margin for the flyers that will be lost or damaged, and no more. A flyer for an ongoing service, with no date on it, can justify a larger run, because the per-flyer saving is real and the flyers will be used before anything changes. A flyer with a price on it should be printed in the quantity that can be used before the price is likely to change. Reprints also deserve a line in the budget. A flyer design that is kept as a proper print-ready file, with the editable source file held by the business or the designer, can be reprinted by any competent printer, with only the printing to pay for. A flyer that exists only as a low-resolution image, or only in a printer's online template system, cannot be reprinted elsewhere without being remade, and the remake will cost design time again. Get the printing quote in writing, for the exact quantity, paper, finish, size and fold, before approving the design. A design approved for a finish or a fold the budget cannot afford will have to be redesigned, and that redesign is the most avoidable cost in the whole flyer project.

General Market Ranges for Flyer Design

The ranges below are what you will typically see across online print services, freelance marketplaces, designer portfolios and studio pricing pages, for the design work on a flyer. They are general market ranges, not quotes, and they do not include printing or distribution unless the text says so. The exact price for your flyer depends on whether it is single-sided, double-sided or folded, on the size, on whether the artwork and photographs are supplied ready to use, on how many design versions and revision rounds are included, and on whether a digital version for sharing is also needed. Treat these as a map, not a price list, and always get a written quote for your exact flyer.

Template or online print-service layout: roughly $0 to around $50, often bundled with printing. You choose a template, type your details into it, and the printer prints the result. The design work is selection and typing, not custom layout, and the result can be perfectly serviceable for a straightforward flyer with a single message and standard details: an event, a sale, a notice. The trade-offs are the ones that apply to any template: the layout is shared with other businesses, the fonts and spacing cannot be adjusted far, the photograph choices are limited, and the file usually stays inside the printer's system, so a reprint elsewhere means starting again. This tier suits a flyer that is needed urgently, a flyer for a short-lived purpose, or a business that wants to test its wording before paying for a custom design. It does not suit a flyer that must look distinct, carry a folded format, use a photograph that needs careful cropping, or match an existing brand precisely.

Custom flyer design by a freelancer: roughly $75 to $400. A designer lays out the flyer for this business alone, working from the supplied artwork, wording and photographs, with one or two design versions and one or two revision rounds, and delivers print-ready files. This is the tier where most small-business flyers land, and it is usually the best value for a standard flyer: the layout is original, the headline is set for the flyer's real size and reading distance, the files are prepared for the printer, and the flyer can be reprinted from those files. The range is wide because a single-sided A5 flyer with supplied artwork and one revision round is a smaller job than a double-sided or folded flyer with photographs to prepare, a code to place, several versions for several locations, and several revision rounds. A flyer that needs the wording shortened, the photographs improved, or a digital version for sharing will sit at the higher end of this tier, and that is fair, because each of those is real work. Check the portfolio for printed flyers, not only screen mockups, and ask whether the price includes the print-ready files, the digital version, and the revision rounds you expect.

Flyer design within a campaign or studio package: roughly $400 to $1,200, usually as part of wider work. A studio rarely prices a single flyer in isolation at this tier. The flyer is designed as part of a campaign — the social media images, the poster, the email header, the window display — with a set process, more than one person checking the work, and a consistent look across every piece. The flyer itself may be one item in a package that costs several hundred dollars or more, and judging it by the flyer alone will make it look expensive. Judged as part of the campaign, it is the cost of consistency: the same headline, colours and images on the flyer, the poster and the social media posts, checked by a team. This tier suits a business that is launching an event, a promotion or a new service and needs the flyer to match everything else from the first day, rather than a business that already has a settled look and only needs one flyer laid out. If the campaign includes photography, copywriting, or several flyer versions for several areas, the price will sit higher in this tier, and the quote should separate those parts so the business can see what it is paying for.

Agency or full campaign: roughly $1,200 and above, as part of a full campaign project. At this level the flyer is one application of a full campaign, designed by a team over weeks, with research, strategy, copywriting, photography and guidelines that govern every place the campaign appears. The flyer's share of that project is not a meaningful number on its own, because the project is buying the campaign, not the flyer. A small business rarely needs this tier for a flyer alone, and it should not buy a full campaign only to get a flyer. It is included here so that the range is honest: the top of the market is not buying a flyer, it is buying a campaign, and the flyer is one small, carefully governed part of it. If a business is quoted a four-figure price for a single flyer with no wider campaign work, ask what the price covers. It may be justified by several versions, several sizes, photography, or a very complex folded format, but it should be explained, not simply stated.

Printing, for comparison, is a separate general market range, and it is worth stating here so that the design ranges above are not mistaken for the total. As a general market range, a standard run of a few hundred plain flyers on a standard paper, with a standard finish, often costs from a few tens of dollars to around one or two hundred dollars, depending on the quantity, the size, the paper and the printer. A run with a fold, a thicker paper, a larger size, or a very small quantity costs more per flyer, and a very large run costs less per flyer than a small run of the same flyer. Those printing figures are broad, they vary by country and by printer, and they are not a quote. The point of stating them is to show the shape of the total: for a standard flyer, the design and the printing are often of a similar order of magnitude, and neither should be budgeted without the other, nor without the distribution. A business that budgets only for the design will be surprised by the printing bill, and a business that budgets only for the printing will be surprised by the design bill, and both will be surprised by the cost of getting the flyers to the reader. Budget for all three, in writing, before the work starts. Our companion post on how much business card design costs in 2026 covers the smaller, permanent card that often accompanies a flyer campaign, and the two should be budgeted together where both are needed, so that the card and the flyer match and the total is known before either is ordered.

What a Flyer Design Package Should Include

A proper flyer design package should be judged by its file list, not by its mockup. The mockup — the flyer shown on a screen, on a desk, or in a hand — is how the design is presented, and it is useful for judging the look. It is not the deliverable. The deliverable is the set of files that lets the flyer be printed correctly, shared correctly, and changed correctly in the future. At a minimum, the package should include a print-ready PDF, made to the printer's specification, with the flyer at its true size, with bleed, with trim marks if the printer asks for them, and in the colour mode the printer requires. Bleed is the extra printed area beyond the flyer's cut edge, usually a few millimetres on each side, which ensures that colour running to the edge is not left with a white hairline if the cutting shifts by a fraction. A file without bleed, for a flyer with colour to the edge, is not print-ready, whatever it is called. The package should also include a screen version of the flyer — usually a JPG or PNG — for email, messaging, and social media, so that the flyer can be shared without sending the print file. And it should state, in writing, how many design versions were offered, how many revision rounds are included, and what happens if a further round is needed.

Beyond the minimum, there are three items worth asking for, and worth paying for if they are not included. The first is the editable source file, or a clear statement of whether it is included and what it may be used for. The source file is the working file the designer built the flyer in, and it is what another designer needs to change a date, a price, or a phone number, or to adapt the flyer to a new size, without rebuilding it. Some designers include it, some charge extra for it, and some retain it and offer small edits as a service. All three arrangements are legitimate, but the arrangement should be known before the work starts, because a flyer without its source file is a flyer that must be remade for every change. The second is the fold artwork, if the flyer is folded: the layout checked against the printer's fold specification, with the panels in the correct order and the fold positions marked, so that the headline does not land on a fold and the panels are read in the order intended. The third is a short written note of the flyer's settings: the true size, the bleed, the colour mode, the fonts used, and the printer's specification the file was made to. That note costs the designer a few minutes and saves the next person — a different printer, a different designer, or the business itself — from guessing. A package that includes the print-ready file, the screen version, the source-file arrangement in writing, and the settings note is a complete package. A package that includes only a screen image, however handsome, is not a package at all. It is a picture of a flyer.

The print-ready file also needs to be right in the technical details that a screen mockup hides. The colour mode matters: print files are usually prepared in CMYK, the four-ink process printers use, while screen files use RGB, the light-based mode screens use, and a file prepared in the wrong mode can print with colours that look duller or shifted from the screen version. The resolution matters: photographs and any raster artwork on the flyer should be at a high resolution at the flyer's true size — commonly 300 dots per inch is quoted as the print standard — because a photograph that looks sharp on a phone screen can look soft or blocky when printed at flyer size if it was supplied too small. The fonts matter: text should be embedded in the PDF or converted to outlines, so that the printer's computer does not substitute a different font when the file is opened. And the safe margin matters: text and important details should sit a few millimetres inside the cut edge and away from any fold, so that a tiny shift in cutting or folding does not clip a letter, a date or a phone number. A designer who checks all four — colour mode, resolution, fonts and safe margin — before delivering the file is doing the part of the job that the client cannot see and the printer will notice immediately. Ask the designer what checks they run before delivery, and ask the printer to confirm the file when it arrives. A printer who says the file is wrong is not being difficult. The printer is the last person who can catch a problem before it is printed a thousand times.

How to Brief a Designer for a Flyer

The fastest way to get an accurate quote — and a good flyer — is a brief that answers the questions the designer will otherwise have to guess. A good flyer brief is short, but it is complete. It states the one message the flyer must carry, in one sentence, because a flyer with one message can be designed and a flyer with five messages cannot. It states the headline, or asks the designer to write one, and says which it is, because a headline written by the designer is a different job from a headline supplied by the client. It lists every detail to be included, in full, with the date and time checked, the address checked to confirm that it is the address the reader should go to, the phone number in the format it should be dialled, and the website address checked to confirm that it loads. It states the offer or the price, if there is one, exactly as it should appear, including any terms that must be shown. It states what should go on the back, or on the inside panels, if anything, in one sentence per side or panel. It states the flyer size, or asks the designer to recommend one, and it states the quantity to be printed, or the quantity range, so that the designer and the printer can both quote the real job. And it states how the flyer will be distributed — handed out, posted, racked, or pinned — because the distribution decides the size, the paper, and the reading distance. A brief that contains those answers will produce a quote that can be compared with another quote for the same job. A brief that omits them will produce quotes that cannot be compared, because each designer will have assumed a different flyer.

The brief should also say what to supply, and in what form. Supply the existing artwork as the best file you have — the original vector or high-resolution file if you have it, not a small image copied from a website or a social media profile, because a small image will not print cleanly at flyer size, however good it looks on a screen. Supply the photographs as the largest files you have, and say which photographs may be cropped, because a photograph that must be used whole will shape the layout differently from one that may be cropped to fit. Supply the colours and fonts if the business has a brand guide, or say that there is no guide and the designer should work from the existing materials. Supply the exact wording, proofread, rather than asking the designer to draft it from a conversation, because a date or a phone number taken down wrong in a conversation will be printed wrong on every flyer. And supply any code, link or handle that must appear on the flyer, tested, so that the designer places a code that works rather than a code that was typed from memory. If the flyer must match a website, it is worth linking the designer to that website in the brief. Our guide to website redesign costs in 2026 is useful context when the flyer and the website are being refreshed together, so that the two are planned as one presence rather than two separate jobs that happen to share a name. The same brief, sent to every designer you contact, is what makes the quotes comparable. Send a different brief to each designer, and the quotes will be for different flyers, however similar they look. What to put on the flyer, and what to leave off, is the hardest part of the brief, because the temptation is to include everything. A flyer that lists every service, every date, every branch and a paragraph about the business is not informative. It is unreadable. The test for every item is simple: will the person who receives this flyer need this item to understand the one message, to decide to act, or to act? A date, a place, a price and a next step pass that test for most flyers. A second offer, a long list of services, and a history of the business do not. Be ruthless with the front. Put the headline, the one message, and the next step. Put the rest, if it earns its place, on the back or inside. And leave enough empty space for the flyer to be read at a glance. A flyer that is read at a glance will be kept. A flyer that must be studied will be put down.

Checking the Proof Before Anything Is Printed

The proof is the last chance to catch an error before it is printed hundreds or thousands of times, and it deserves the same care as the design itself. When the designer sends the proof, check it in the order a stranger would read it, not in the order you wrote the brief. Read the headline first, and ask whether a stranger would understand it without knowing the business. Read the date and the time digit by digit, and compare them with the booking, the venue, or the calendar, not with your memory of when the event is. Read the address letter by letter, and check it on a map. Read the phone number digit by digit, and dial it, or have someone else dial it, to confirm that it reaches the right person. Read the email address letter by letter, and send a test message to it. Open the website address from the proof, by typing it exactly as it appears, and confirm that it loads the right page. Scan any code on the proof with a phone, from a printed copy if possible, and confirm that it opens the right page. Check any price, any offer, and any terms in the same way, against a source that is not the brief itself, because an error copied from the brief into the design will survive every check that only compares the proof with the brief. Two people checking the proof is better than one, because the person who wrote the wording will read what they meant to write, while a fresh reader will read what is actually there. Give the proof a night if you can. An error that is invisible at midnight is often obvious in the morning, and a print run ordered in haste is a print run that cannot be recalled.

Beyond the wording, check the proof as a printed object. Ask the designer or the printer for a proof at the true size, on paper if possible, and hold it at the distance at which it will be read — at arm's length for a handout flyer, from a step back for a flyer that will be pinned to a board. Is the headline readable at that distance without squinting? Is the smallest text readable, or is it so small that it will be ignored? Is there enough space inside the cut edge, and away from any fold, that a tiny shift in cutting or folding would not clip a letter or a date? Does the back align with the front in the way it should, and do the folded panels open in the order intended? Does the colour on the proof match the colour you expected, allowing for the fact that a screen and a printed sheet never match exactly? If the flyer uses a photograph, is it sharp at the true size, or does it break up when seen on paper? None of these checks takes long, and together they take far less time than reprinting a run. Approve the proof in writing, with the date, so that there is a clear record of what was approved. A written approval protects both sides: the printer knows the file that was approved, the designer knows the design that was approved, and the business knows the flyer that was ordered. If the printed flyers differ from the approved proof, the written approval is the document that settles the question. If there was no written approval, the question is settled by memory, and memory is a poor record for a print job.

Red Flags and Smart Ways to Save

A few warning signs are worth knowing before you pay anyone. Be cautious of a designer who will not show printed flyers they have designed, or whose examples are all screen mockups, because a flyer that has never been printed has never been tested where it matters. Be cautious of a quote that does not say whether the price includes print-ready files, how many revision rounds are included, whether a digital version is included, or whether the source file is included, because those omissions are where cheap quotes usually hide their limits. Be cautious of a price that includes no proofing step, or a printer who will print without a proof, because the proof is the only protection against a printed error. Be cautious of artwork supplied only as a small image file, with no check of its resolution, because the flyer will be printed from that file and the result will show. Be cautious of a flyer design that must be re-bought for every date, price change, or location, with no arrangement for small edits, because a business that runs regular events will pay the full design fee several times for what should be a small edit. And be cautious of anyone who promises that a flyer alone will bring customers. A flyer can help a business be noticed and remembered, but it cannot, on its own, bring the customers. None of these mean the designer or printer is dishonest. They usually mean the package is smaller than it looks, and you should check before you buy.

As for saving money without cutting corners, the best saving is a complete brief, because it avoids revision rounds spent discovering what the flyer should have said. The second is to settle the details before ordering: a smaller first run, with the wording tested in use, costs less in the long run than a large run of a flyer whose date, price or phone number changes a week later. The third is to buy the design once and reprint from the same files, rather than paying for a new layout for every reprint or every small change — ask, before the first flyer is designed, what a date change or a price change will cost. The fourth is to choose the size and the finish honestly: a standard size and a standard finish, well designed, will serve most businesses better than a special size or finish on a flyer whose headline is crowded or whose text is too small. The fifth is to bundle the flyer with the other pieces the campaign needs, if it needs them — the poster, the social media images, the window display — because a designer who already knows the campaign can produce those faster and more consistently than a new designer starting from scratch, and the set will match. And the sixth is timing: a flyer ordered without a deadline, with the proof checked properly, will cost less in reprints than a flyer ordered in a rush, approved without checking, and printed twice. A flyer is a small purchase. Treated as a small purchase, it will be bought twice. Treated as the direct, physical introduction it actually is, it will be bought once, and bought well. Frequently Asked Questions

How much does flyer design cost in 2026?
As a general market range, a template-based flyer layout typically costs very little or nothing beyond the printing, a custom single-sided flyer from a freelancer typically runs from roughly seventy-five dollars to a few hundred dollars, and a flyer designed as part of a wider campaign or studio package typically costs more because the price covers the wider work as well. The design fee is usually a one-off cost, separate from printing, and the total depends on the size, whether the flyer is single-sided or double-sided or folded, the number of design versions, and the revision rounds included. Always get a written quote for your exact flyer before budgeting from a range.

Is flyer design charged separately from printing?
Usually, yes. Design and printing are two different jobs, and they are usually charged separately. The design fee pays for the layout, the headline, the arrangement of the message, and the print-ready files. The printing fee pays for the paper, the finish, the cutting and folding, and the number of flyers produced. Some printers bundle a basic template layout with the print order, and some designers include a first print run in their package, so check every quote to see whether printing is included or whether you will pay the printer separately.

Does a double-sided or folded flyer cost more than a single-sided one?
Yes, usually. A double-sided flyer is a second layout to design, balance and proof, even when the back is simpler than the front, and a folded flyer is several panels that must work in order and fold correctly. Printing also usually costs more, because both sides must be printed and aligned, and a fold adds a finishing step. The difference is often modest for a standard double-sided flyer, but it grows when the flyer is folded, carries photographs on several panels, or uses a special finish. Decide what each side and each panel must do before you ask for a quote, so the designer can price the real job.

What files should I receive for a flyer?
You should receive a print-ready PDF with bleed, trim marks and the correct colour mode for the printer you will use, plus a screen version for email, messaging or social media. Ask for the exact flyer size, the bleed allowance, the resolution and the colour mode to be confirmed in writing before the files are made, because a file made for one printer or one size may not suit another. Keep the print-ready file, the screen version, and any editable source file that the package includes, so a future reprint or a small change does not mean paying for the whole design again.
