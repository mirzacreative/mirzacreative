Website Redesign Case Study: Doubling Enquiries

November 18, 2026 • 8 min read • By Muhammad Atif Iqbal

A website can look fine and still fail. It loads, the colours are pleasant, nothing is obviously broken — and yet the phone rarely rings, the contact form sits empty, and the owner wonders whether the website is worth the money. That is the situation this case study walks through: a typical small business website that was not bringing in enquiries, the audit that found out why, the redesign decisions that fixed it, and the before-and-after structure of the new site. One thing to be clear about up front: this is an illustrative walkthrough, not a report on a real client. No client is named, no testimonial is quoted, and no measured results are claimed — the example is built from the patterns that turn up again and again in small business websites, and the "doubling enquiries" in the title is the goal the redesign was designed around, not a measured result from a real project. The value of the walkthrough is the process: the same audit, the same decisions, and the same before-and-after structure apply to almost any small business site that is not earning its keep.

In this example, the business is a typical local service business — the kind with a handful of services, a service area, and customers who find it through Google or a recommendation. The old site had been built years earlier, added to in pieces, and never properly reviewed. The owner believed the problem was traffic: not enough people were visiting the site. The audit showed something different. The traffic was reasonable for a local business; the problem was what happened after people arrived. Most visitors left within seconds, few reached a service page, and almost nobody reached the contact form. The redesign was therefore not about getting more visitors. It was about turning the visitors who were already arriving into enquiries. That distinction — traffic versus conversion — is the first decision in any redesign, and getting it wrong means spending money on the wrong problem.

The Audit: Finding What Was Actually Broken

Every redesign should start with an audit, and the audit should start with the visitor, not the design. In this example, the audit had four parts. First, the analytics: which pages people visited, how long they stayed, and where they left. The homepage got most of the traffic, the services pages got very little, and the contact page was barely visited at all — which suggested visitors were not finding their way to the pages that could sell to them. Second, the mobile check: the site was tested on a phone, the way most visitors would see it. The text was small, the menu was hard to use, the phone number was not tappable, and the contact form required pinching and zooming to fill in. Third, the speed check: the homepage loaded slowly, mostly because of large, uncompressed photos, which meant visitors on mobile data were waiting before they saw anything. Fourth, the content check: every page was read as a customer would read it. The homepage did not say what the business did in the first screen. The services were listed by internal names the customer would not recognise. There were no prices, no service area, no proof — no photos of real work, no reviews, no guarantees. The contact form asked for too much information up front. Put together, the audit told a clear story: the site was not broken in the technical sense, but it was not doing the job a business website exists to do — turning a visitor into an enquiry.

What Was Broken: Five Problems, In Order of Impact

1. The homepage did not say what the business did. The first screen showed a large photo and a slogan — "Quality you can trust" — with no mention of the service, the area, or who it was for. A visitor arriving from Google had to scroll to work out whether they were in the right place, and most did not bother. A homepage has a few seconds to answer three questions: what do you do, who is it for, and where do you do it. In this example, it answered none of them.

2. The services were hidden. The services were listed on a single page, under internal names, with no detail on each one. A visitor looking for one specific service could not tell whether the business offered it, what it involved, or what it cost. Each service needed its own page, written in the customer's words, with the details a customer actually asks about.

3. There was no reason to trust the business. The site had no photos of real work, no customer reviews, no "about" story, and no guarantees. A visitor deciding between this business and a competitor had nothing to go on except the price — and the site did not show prices either. Trust has to be built on the page, because the visitor cannot meet the business first.

4. The site did not work on a phone. Most of the traffic came from phones, but the site had been designed for a desktop. Text was too small, buttons were too close together, the phone number could not be tapped to call, and the contact form was painful to complete. Every one of those problems cost enquiries, because a visitor who cannot tap to call will simply call the next business in the search results.

5. The contact form asked too much. The form had eight fields, including a full address and a preferred contact time, before the visitor had even decided to enquire. Every extra field is a reason to abandon the form. A first enquiry needs a name, a phone number or email, and a short message — nothing more.

The Redesign Decisions

Homepage first, and only the essentials. The new homepage opens with a clear headline that names the service, the area and the customer: one sentence, no slogan. Below it, a short line of proof — years in business, or the number of jobs completed in the area — and two buttons: "Get a quote" and "Call now". Everything else on the homepage earns its place by answering a visitor's question: the services in plain words, a few photos of real work, a short strip of customer reviews, and the service area. Nothing decorative, nothing that does not help a visitor decide.

One page per service. Each service got its own page, written in the words customers use, with the same structure every time: what the service is, who it is for, what is included, what it typically costs, and how long it takes. Each page ends with the same call to action — the short contact form or the phone number. This structure does two jobs: it helps the visitor decide, and it helps Google understand what the business offers, because each page is about one clear topic instead of a list of everything.

Trust built into every page. Real photos of completed work replaced the stock images. Customer reviews were added to the homepage and to the service pages, with names and areas. An "about" section explained who runs the business and how long it has been going. And the guarantees were stated plainly: response times, warranties, and what happens if something goes wrong. None of this is decoration — it is the evidence a visitor needs before they pick up the phone.

Mobile first, not mobile afterthought. The new site was designed for the phone screen first and the desktop second. Text is readable without zooming, buttons are large enough to tap, the phone number is a tappable link on every page, and the contact form is three fields and a submit button. The photos were compressed so the homepage loads quickly on mobile data. If a design choice looked good on a desktop but was awkward on a phone, the phone won — because that is where most of the visitors are.

A contact form that respects the visitor. The new form asks for a name, a contact detail, and a short message — three fields. The submit button says "Request a call back" rather than "Submit", so the visitor knows what happens next. And the form is reachable from every page, not just the contact page, because a visitor who has decided to enquire should not have to go looking for the way to do it.

Before and After: The Structure, Side by Side

Before. A homepage with a slogan and a large photo, no clear statement of the service or the area. One services page listing everything under internal names. No photos of real work, no reviews, no guarantees. A site designed for desktop that was hard to use on a phone. A contact page with an eight-field form, buried two clicks from the homepage. No prices, no service area, and no reason for a visitor to trust the business over its competitors.

After. A homepage that names the service, the area and the customer in the first screen, with a clear call to action and a line of proof. A separate page for each service, written in the customer's words, with what is included, typical costs, and the same call to action at the end. Real photos of completed work and customer reviews on the homepage and the service pages. A site designed for the phone first, with a tappable phone number on every page and a three-field contact form. Fast-loading pages, clear navigation, and a contact form that is never more than one tap away. The structure is the same idea as the old site — homepage, services, about, contact — but every page now has a job, and every job is to turn a visitor into an enquiry.

What to Measure After Launch

A redesign is not finished at launch; it is finished when the numbers confirm it works. In this example, the numbers to watch were enquiries, not traffic: the number of contact-form submissions, phone calls and quote requests, counted over the same length of time before and after launch. Traffic alone would have been misleading — a redesign that brings more visitors but the same number of enquiries has failed. The secondary numbers were the ones that explain the enquiries: the percentage of homepage visitors who reach a service page, the percentage of mobile visitors who reach the contact form, and the time visitors spend on the service pages. If enquiries rise and those secondary numbers rise with them, the redesign is working for the reasons it was designed to work. If enquiries do not rise, the numbers show where the new site is still losing people — and that is the next thing to fix. One warning: give the new site at least a month before judging it. Search rankings take time to settle after a redesign, and a week's data is mostly noise. Compare a full month before launch with a full month after, and judge the redesign on enquiries, not on how the site looks.

Lessons From the Walkthrough

Audit before you design. The owner of the site in this example believed the problem was traffic. The audit showed the problem was conversion — visitors were arriving and leaving. A redesign that had chased more traffic would have spent money on the wrong problem. The audit takes a few days and saves weeks of wasted work.

Structure beats decoration. The new site does not win because it looks prettier — it wins because every page has a job, the services are findable, the trust evidence is visible, and the contact form is easy to reach. A redesign that only changes the colours and the photos is a refresh, not a redesign, and it will not fix a site whose structure is the problem.

Mobile is the main screen. Most small business websites get most of their visitors on a phone. Designing for the desktop first and adapting for the phone afterwards is backwards. Design for the phone, and the desktop will take care of itself.

Keep the URLs that matter. A redesign that changes URLs without redirecting the old ones will lose the search rankings the old pages earned. Plan the URL map before launch, keep the pages that ranked, and redirect anything that moves. This is the one redesign mistake that is expensive to fix afterwards and cheap to prevent.

Measure enquiries, not traffic. A website exists to bring in business. Traffic is a means to that end, not the end itself. Judge a redesign by the enquiries it produces, and keep measuring for at least a month before deciding whether it worked.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does a website redesign usually take?
A typical small business redesign takes a few weeks from audit to launch, depending on the size of the site and how much content needs rewriting. The audit and planning stage usually takes the first week, design and build take the next two or three, and testing and launch take the last few days. A site with many pages or an online shop takes longer.

Will a redesign hurt my Google rankings?
A redesign can hurt rankings if URLs change without redirects, or if pages that ranked well are removed. Done properly — keeping the URLs that matter, redirecting any that change, and keeping the content that earned the rankings — a redesign usually helps rankings, because the new site is faster, clearer and easier to use. Always plan the URL map before launch.

What should I measure after a redesign?
Measure enquiries, not just traffic. Track the number of contact-form submissions, phone calls and quote requests before and after launch, over the same length of time. Also watch bounce rate on the homepage, time on the services pages, and the percentage of mobile visitors who reach the contact form — those numbers show whether the new design is actually working.

Do I need a full redesign or just a refresh?
A refresh is enough when the structure is sound and only the look is dated: new colours, better photos, clearer text. A full redesign is needed when the structure itself is the problem — visitors cannot find the services, the contact form is buried, or the site does not work on a phone. The audit stage of a redesign project will tell you which one you need.
